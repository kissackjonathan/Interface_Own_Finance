Part 1 · In briefGeography is destiny — or is it?

Geopolitics is the study of how geography — mountains, rivers, seas, distance, climate, resources, population — shapes the power and the choices of states. Why does Russia always seek buffer zones? Why is the United States so secure? Why does China fear the Strait of Malacca? Why is Switzerland neutral? Behind each answer there is a map.

Geopolitical thought is the family of ideas that tries to explain these patterns: from ancient Greek historians and Chinese strategists to the British geographer who imagined a "Heartland" ruling the world, and the modern theories of realism, liberalism and constructivism. Investors who know these ideas read the news faster: they recognise old patterns behind new headlines.

The one idea to remember

Geography does not change, but technology, economics and ideas change what geography means. Oceans protected the US in 1900; missiles reduced that protection. The Strait of Hormuz mattered little before oil; today it can shake the world economy. A good geopolitical analyst always asks three questions: What does the map force this country to fear? What resources does it need? What can it actually do (power)?

Part 2 · History of ideasFrom Thucydides to weaponised interdependence

  1. ~5th c. BCThucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War: Athens' rise and Sparta's fear made war likely; in the "Melian dialogue", the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must — the founding text of realism.
  2. ~5th c. BCSun Tzu, The Art of War (China): win without fighting, know the enemy and yourself, use terrain.
  3. ~3rd c. BCKautilya, Arthashastra (India): the "mandala" theory — your neighbour is your natural enemy, your neighbour's neighbour your friend.
  4. 1513Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince: politics as the pursuit and preservation of power.
  5. 1648Peace of Westphalia: the system of sovereign states and the balance of power.
  6. 1651Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan: without a sovereign, life is a "war of all against all" — the image of international anarchy.
  7. 1748Montesquieu: climate and geography shape laws and peoples' character (an idea later abused).
  8. 1795Immanuel Kant, Perpetual Peace: republics, trade and law can bring peace — the root of liberalism.
  9. 1832Carl von Clausewitz, On War: war is the continuation of politics by other means.
  10. 1890Alfred Thayer Mahan, The Influence of Sea Power upon History: control of the seas and naval bases makes great powers (inspired the US, German and Japanese navies).
  11. 1897–1899Friedrich Ratzel sees the state as a living organism needing space; the Swede Rudolf Kjellén coins the word "geopolitics".
  12. 1904Halford Mackinder, "The Geographical Pivot of History": the Heartland of Eurasia, unreachable by sea power, is the pivot of world power. In 1919: who rules Eastern Europe commands the Heartland; who rules the Heartland commands the World-Island; who rules the World-Island commands the world.
  13. 1920s–40sKarl Haushofer's German Geopolitik (Lebensraum) is used by Nazi propaganda — geopolitics becomes a suspect word for decades.
  14. 1942–44Nicholas Spykman: the Rimland (the coastal belt of Eurasia: Europe, Middle East, South and East Asia) matters more than the Heartland. Basis of US Cold War strategy.
  15. 1946–47George Kennan's "Long Telegram" and "X article": containment of the Soviet Union.
  16. 1948Hans Morgenthau, Politics Among Nations: classical realism — national interest defined as power.
  17. 1962Raymond Aron, Peace and War Among Nations: a French synthesis of sociology, history and strategy.
  18. 1976Yves Lacoste revives French geopolitics (journal Hérodote): his book argues geography serves first of all to make war.
  19. 1977–79Keohane & Nye (complex interdependence) and Kenneth Waltz (neorealism: the structure of the system — anarchy and the distribution of power — drives behaviour).
  20. 1989–92Francis Fukuyama: "The End of History" — liberal democracy as the final form of government.
  21. 1990Joseph Nye: soft power — attraction (culture, values, institutions) as a form of power.
  22. 1992Alexander Wendt: "anarchy is what states make of it" — constructivism.
  23. 1993–96Samuel Huntington: the "Clash of Civilizations" — future conflicts along cultural lines.
  24. 1997Zbigniew Brzezinski, The Grand Chessboard: US primacy depends on preventing a hostile power from dominating Eurasia; Ukraine as a geopolitical pivot.
  25. 2001John Mearsheimer, The Tragedy of Great Power Politics: offensive realism — great powers seek regional hegemony.
  26. 2014Henry Kissinger, World Order: different civilisations have different ideas of order.
  27. 2017Graham Allison: the Thucydides Trap — when a rising power threatens a ruling power, war often follows (US–China).
  28. 2019Henry Farrell & Abraham Newman: weaponised interdependence — the powers that control global networks (dollar, SWIFT, internet, chips) can use them as weapons.

3.2 The key concepts, in plain words

Power (hard, soft, smart)
Hard: military and economic coercion. Soft: attraction. Smart: the mix.
Balance of power
States ally against whoever becomes too strong (Britain's policy towards Europe for centuries).
Hegemony
One power dominates and sets the rules (Pax Britannica, Pax Americana).
Polarity
Number of great powers: bipolar (Cold War), unipolar (1990s–2000s), multipolar (emerging today).
Security dilemma
When one state arms for defence, its neighbour feels threatened and arms too — spirals of mistrust.
Deterrence
Preventing attack by threatening unacceptable costs; nuclear "mutually assured destruction".
Sphere of influence
Region where a great power claims priority (Monroe Doctrine 1823 for the Americas; Russia's "near abroad").
Buffer state
A country between rivals that absorbs pressure (Belgium, Afghanistan, Mongolia, historically Switzerland in part).
Strategic depth
Distance between front lines and the core — the reason Russia valued Eastern Europe.
Heartland / Rimland
Mackinder's continental core of Eurasia vs Spykman's coastal belt.
Sea power vs land power
Maritime trading empires (Britain, US) vs continental empires (Russia, Germany, China historically).
Chokepoint
Narrow passage on a vital route (Hormuz, Malacca, Suez, Bosphorus, Panama, Gibraltar, Taiwan Strait).
Island chains
Lines of islands (Japan–Taiwan–Philippines) that contain China's navy.
Thucydides Trap
Risk of war between a rising and a ruling power.
Geoeconomics
Using economic tools (sanctions, tariffs, investment, export controls) for geopolitical goals.
Resource curse
Countries rich in oil or minerals often have weaker institutions, corruption and conflict (Venezuela, Nigeria).
Neutrality
A state that stays out of wars between others (Switzerland, recognised 1815; codified by the Hague Conventions of 1907).
Realpolitik
Policy based on interests and power rather than ideals.

3.4 The permanent factors to analyse

  • Relief and borders: mountains protect (Afghanistan, Switzerland), plains expose (Poland).
  • Rivers and water: navigable rivers create wealth; shared rivers create tension (Nile and Ethiopia's GERD dam, Indus between India and Pakistan, Mekong, Tigris–Euphrates).
  • Seas and ports: access to the sea, warm-water ports, naval bases.
  • Resources: energy, minerals, food, fresh water.
  • Demography: size, age, migration (aging China, Europe and Japan; young Africa and India).
  • Climate: melting Arctic opens routes and resources; droughts drive migration and conflict.
  • Technology: changes distances and vulnerabilities (missiles, drones, cyber, satellites, AI).
  • Identity and history: religion, language, historical memory — maps of the mind.
Using geopolitical thought as an investor

(1) Identify the constant (geography, demography) and the variable (leaders, technology, alliances). (2) Use several lenses: a realist reading (power and fear) predicts escalation; a liberal reading (trade, institutions) predicts restraint — markets swing between the two. (3) Look for the chokepoint and the resource in any crisis. (4) Remember that geography changes slowly but regimes can change in a night (Iran, February 2026; Venezuela, January 2026).

A game to play

When a crisis appears in the news, find the nearest city in this list and ask: which route, resource or border does it control? Which assets would move (oil, gas, grain, chips, shipping, defence, currencies)? You will be surprised how often the answer appears within minutes.

Part 5 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. What is the difference between Mackinder's Heartland and Spykman's Rimland?

The Heartland is the continental interior of Eurasia; the Rimland is the coastal belt around it. Mackinder saw the Heartland as the key to world power, Spykman the Rimland.

2. Why does Russia historically seek buffer zones in Eastern Europe?

The North European Plain offers no natural barriers; strategic depth protects Moscow from invasion (Napoleon, Hitler).

3. What is the Thucydides Trap?

The tendency for a rising power and a ruling power to end up in conflict, because of the ruling power's fear.

4. Why is Gwadar strategic for China?

It offers a route to the Arabian Sea and the Gulf through Pakistan, avoiding the Strait of Malacca.

5. What is weaponised interdependence?

Using control of global networks (finance, tech, supply chains) to pressure other states.
