Part 1 · In briefPrice is what you pay, value is what you get

Warren Buffett's famous line sums up this course. The price is a fact: it is on the screen. The value is an estimate: what the asset is really worth given the cash it will produce and the risks involved. Valuation is the craft of building that estimate in a disciplined, honest way — and of understanding why the market's price may be different.

There are four big families of valuation methods:

  • Intrinsic valuation (DCF): value = present value of future cash flows. The most fundamental approach.
  • Relative valuation (multiples): value = what similar assets trade at (P/E, EV/EBITDA…). The most used in practice.
  • Transaction-based valuation: what buyers have paid for whole companies (precedent transactions) or could pay using debt (LBO analysis).
  • Contingent-claim valuation (options): some assets behave like options — a patent, an undeveloped oil field, the equity of a company close to bankruptcy.
The one idea to remember

Every valuation is a story turned into numbers. A DCF is not more "true" than a multiple — it simply makes your assumptions explicit. The best valuation is not the most precise one; it is the one whose assumptions are realistic, consistent with each other, and clearly linked to how the business actually works.

The Stock Analysis course introduced the DCF, WACC and multiples. This course goes further: how to choose every input, how to handle difficult cases (banks, start-ups, cyclicals, emerging markets, private companies), how private equity values a company with an LBO, how option theory helps, and how professionals combine all methods into one conclusion.

Part 2 · HistoryHow valuation became a science

  1. 1930Irving Fisher, The Theory of Interest: the value of any capital asset is the present value of its future income.
  2. 1938John Burr Williams applies it to stocks: value = present value of future dividends.
  3. 1956Gordon and Shapiro: the constant-growth formula \(P = D_1/(r-g)\).
  4. 1958–61Modigliani and Miller: without taxes and frictions, value depends on cash flows, not on financing or dividend policy. With taxes, debt creates a tax shield — the basis of WACC and APV.
  5. 1964CAPM (Sharpe and others) gives a way to estimate the discount rate.
  6. 1973Black, Scholes and Merton price options. In 1977 Stewart Myers coins the term "real options" for investment opportunities inside companies.
  7. 1974Stewart Myers introduces Adjusted Present Value (APV): value the business as if unlevered, then add the value of the tax shield.
  8. 1976KKR is founded. The 1980s leveraged buyout boom culminates in the RJR Nabisco deal (1988–89, about 25 billion dollars) — made famous by the book Barbarians at the Gate.
  9. 1990McKinsey consultants Copeland, Koller and Murrin publish Valuation, which becomes the standard corporate finance reference. Stern Stewart promotes EVA.
  10. 1995James Ohlson formalises the residual income model, linking value directly to accounting numbers.
  11. 1999–2000The dot-com bubble: companies with no profits are valued on "eyeballs" and "clicks". When it bursts, the Nasdaq loses about 78% from peak to trough — a lesson in narratives disconnected from cash flows.
  12. 2000sAswath Damodaran (NYU) publishes free data on equity risk premiums, betas and multiples for every industry and country; his implied ERP becomes a market standard.
  13. 2006–13Fair value accounting standards (US FAS 157, then IFRS 13) define a hierarchy of valuation inputs: Level 1 (quoted prices), Level 2 (observable inputs), Level 3 (models). Valuation enters the balance sheet.
  14. 2010s–20sLow rates push valuations of long-duration growth assets very high; the 2022 rate shock brings them down sharply. Private markets and venture capital grow enormously, raising new valuation questions.

Part 3 · The coreThe full machinery of valuation

3.1 The valuation process

  1. Define the question: value for whom and for what? A minority stake in a listed company (market value), a controlling stake (control value), a liquidation, a tax or legal case. The "standard of value" changes the answer.
  2. Understand the business and write the story: market size, growth, competitive position, margins at maturity, reinvestment needs, risks. Damodaran's test: the story must be possible, plausible and probable.
  3. Translate the story into numbers: revenue growth, target margin, sales-to-capital ratio (reinvestment), risk (discount rate), probability of failure.
  4. Value with several methods (DCF, multiples, transactions, LBO where relevant).
  5. Check consistency and sensitivity; compare with the market price; explain the gap.
  6. Conclude with a range and the key drivers that would change it.

3.2 The discount rate in depth

Risk-free rate: match the currency

The risk-free rate must be in the same currency as the cash flows and roughly match their duration. A DCF in CHF uses the Swiss Confederation bond yield (very low); a DCF in USD uses US Treasuries. Mixing a CHF risk-free rate with USD cash flows (or with USD inflation in the growth rate) is a classic error. Rule: inflation in the cash flows must equal inflation in the discount rate.

Equity risk premium (ERP): historical vs implied

  • Historical ERP: average past excess return of stocks over bonds (about 4–6% depending on country and period). Very noisy and backward-looking.
  • Implied ERP (Damodaran): take today's index level and expected cash returns to shareholders (dividends + buybacks) growing at expected rates, then solve for the discount rate that makes their present value equal the index. ERP = that rate − risk-free rate. It is forward-looking and changes every day with the market.
\[ \text{Index level} = \sum_{t=1}^{5}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{CF_5\,(1+g)}{(r-g)(1+r)^5} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{ERP}_{\text{implied}} = r - r_f \]

Country risk premium

Companies operating in riskier countries deserve a higher discount rate. A common method:

\[ \text{CRP} = \text{Sovereign default spread} \times \frac{\sigma_{\text{equity market}}}{\sigma_{\text{government bond}}} \]

Example: a country's dollar bonds trade 2% above US Treasuries; its equity market is 1.5 times as volatile as its bonds → CRP ≈ 3%. Apply it according to where the company operates (revenues), not where it is listed.

Beta: bottom-up is better

A single company's regression beta has a large standard error. Better: take the average unlevered beta of many comparable firms, then relever with the target company's own debt-to-equity ratio:

\[ \beta_U = \frac{\beta_L}{1 + (1-t)\,D/E} \qquad\qquad \beta_L = \beta_U\left[1 + (1-t)\,\frac{D}{E}\right] \]

Cost of debt

Use the yield on the company's long-term bonds, or a synthetic rating: estimate the rating from the interest coverage ratio (EBIT / interest), then add the typical spread for that rating to the risk-free rate. After-tax cost of debt = \(r_D(1-t)\). Leases are debt.

Weights

Use market values (or target capital structure), not book values. A company with a book equity of 10 and a market cap of 200 is mostly financed by equity, whatever its balance sheet says.

Size premium and "fudge factors"

Many practitioners add a "size premium" or a "company-specific premium" of several percent to the cost of equity of small firms. Be careful: the evidence for a size premium has weakened a lot since the 1980s, and arbitrary premiums often hide uncertainty that should be in the cash flows (scenarios, probability of failure) instead.

3.3 Cash flows in depth

  • Start from revenue, not profit: revenue growth × target operating margin → EBIT → NOPAT. The margin path towards maturity is often the most important assumption.
  • Reinvestment through the sales-to-capital ratio: \(\text{Reinvestment}_t = \Delta\text{Revenue}_t / (\text{Sales/Capital})\). It forces growth to be paid for and keeps ROIC realistic.
  • Maintenance vs growth capex: a mature firm still needs capex roughly equal to depreciation (more with inflation).
  • Normalise: remove one-offs, use mid-cycle margins for cyclicals.
  • Stock-based compensation is a real expense: keep it in costs (do not add it back), or you will overvalue companies that pay employees in shares.
  • R&D is an investment: Damodaran capitalises it (adds the research asset to invested capital and amortises it), which raises both earnings and invested capital and gives a truer ROIC for tech and pharma.
  • Mid-year convention: cash flows arrive during the year, not at its end; discount at \(t - 0.5\). This adds roughly half a year of WACC to the value.

3.4 Terminal value: where most of the value lives

Three ways, which should be consistent:

Formulas · Terminal value \[ TV_N = \frac{\text{FCFF}_{N+1}}{\text{WACC} - g} \qquad TV_N = \frac{\text{NOPAT}_{N+1}\,(1 - g/\text{RONIC})}{\text{WACC} - g} \qquad TV_N = \text{EBITDA}_N \times \text{Exit multiple} \]

The second (value-driver) version is the most honest: it forces reinvestment to match growth. Typical assumptions for a mature company: \(g\) ≤ long-term risk-free rate (or nominal GDP growth), RONIC close to WACC for average firms (competition erodes excess returns) or somewhat above for firms with lasting moats.

  • If the terminal value is 75–85% of the total, that is normal for a growing company — but it means your long-term assumptions dominate.
  • Check the implied exit multiple of your Gordon terminal value and the implied growth of your exit multiple. If a 2% growth rate implies an exit EV/EBITDA of 25×, something is inconsistent.

3.6 Relative valuation done properly

  1. Choose true comparables: similar growth, risk, margins and reinvestment needs — not just "same industry".
  2. Use consistent definitions: same period (next 12 months), same accounting adjustments, EV with EV-based measures.
  3. Look at the distribution: use the median, or the harmonic mean for ratios like P/E (it reduces the effect of very high multiples).
  4. Control for fundamentals: a simple regression across peers — e.g. EV/EBITDA against expected growth and ROIC — tells you what multiple a company "deserves" given its fundamentals.
  5. Explain the gap: is the company cheaper because it is worse, or because the market is wrong?

Trading comparables use market prices of listed peers (minority stakes). Precedent transactions use prices paid in acquisitions; they include a control premium (often 20–40% above the pre-announcement price) because the buyer gains control and expects synergies.

3.7 Sum of the parts and holding discounts

For conglomerates and holding companies, value each division separately (with its own multiple or DCF), add them, subtract net debt and central costs (capitalised). The market often prices conglomerates below their sum of the parts — a conglomerate or holding discount of 10–30% — because of complexity, capital misallocation, taxes on a break-up and lack of control. Activists often target such discounts and push for spin-offs.

Example: the VC method

A start-up could be worth CHF 500 million at exit in 5 years. The VC fund targets a 50% annual return (to compensate for the many failures). Post-money value today = 500 / 1.5⁵ ≈ CHF 66 million. If the fund invests CHF 10 million, it asks for about 10/66 ≈ 15% of the company.

3.9 LBO analysis: how private equity values a company

In a leveraged buyout, a private equity fund buys a company using a lot of debt, uses the company's cash flows to repay that debt, improves the business, and sells after 3–7 years. The LBO model answers: what is the maximum price we can pay and still reach our target return (often 20%+ IRR)? It gives a "floor" valuation for any company that could be bought by a financial buyer.

Example: a simple LBO

Entry: EBITDA 100, purchase at 10× → EV 1,000, financed with 600 of debt (6× EBITDA, 6% interest) and 400 of equity.
Operations: EBITDA grows 5% per year; 50% of EBITDA becomes cash before interest; after-tax interest is paid and all remaining cash repays debt.

If the fund could also exit at 11×, equity would be about 963 → IRR ≈ 19%. This is why PE funds love buying cheap and selling higher ("multiple arbitrage") — and why higher interest rates (more costly debt, lower exit multiples) hurt them.

3.10 Real options and option-based valuation

Some value comes from flexibility: the right, but not the obligation, to do something later. A DCF based on one expected scenario misses it.

  • Option to expand: a first factory that opens the door to a big market.
  • Option to delay: an undeveloped oil field or a patent — you invest only if prices or demand become attractive.
  • Option to abandon: selling or closing a project if it fails limits losses.
Formula · Black-Scholes (call option) \[ C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)\,T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} \]

For a real option: \(S\) = present value of the project's cash flows, \(K\) = investment cost, \(T\) = time you can wait (e.g. patent life), \(\sigma\) = volatility of project value, \(r\) = risk-free rate, \(N(\cdot)\) = normal cumulative distribution.

Example: a patent

A drug patent gives 5 years to launch a product. Present value of the product's cash flows today: 100; launch cost: 120; volatility 40%; risk-free 3%. A static NPV says 100 − 120 = −20: worthless. But the right to wait and launch only if things go well is worth \(d_1 = 0.411\), \(d_2 = -0.483\), \(N(d_1) = 0.659\), \(N(d_2) = 0.314\) → \(C = 100 \times 0.659 - 120\,e^{-0.15} \times 0.314 \approx\) 33.5. Flexibility turns a "bad" project into a valuable asset.

Equity as an option: for a very indebted company, equity is a call option on the firm's assets with a strike equal to the face value of debt (Merton). Even if assets are worth less than the debt today, equity still has value because of the chance that assets recover — which is why shares of near-bankrupt companies rarely trade at zero.

3.11 Triangulation and the "football field"

Professionals present the result as a chart of value ranges for each method — the football field: 52-week trading range, analyst targets, trading comparables, precedent transactions, DCF (with sensitivity), LBO. The final conclusion is a range where the most reliable methods overlap, with a clear explanation of which method is weighted most and why.

The most common valuation mistakes
  • Growth without reinvestment (or ROIC rising to unrealistic levels in the forecast).
  • Inconsistent inflation or currency between cash flows and discount rate.
  • Terminal growth above the risk-free rate or nominal GDP growth.
  • Adding back stock-based compensation; ignoring dilution, leases or pensions.
  • Double-counting risk (higher discount rate and pessimistic cash flows for the same risk).
  • Bias: starting from the answer the client or your thesis wants, then adjusting inputs. Fix: decide assumptions before seeing the result; have someone challenge them.
  • False precision: valuing to the cent something whose uncertainty is ±30%.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. You value a Swiss company with cash flows in CHF but use a 4% US Treasury yield as the risk-free rate. What is wrong?

Currency mismatch. CHF cash flows embed Swiss inflation, which is much lower; the discount rate must use a CHF risk-free rate. Otherwise the company is undervalued.

2. A company has 50 million shares and 5 million options with strike 40; the share trades at 80. Diluted shares with the treasury stock method?

50 + 5 × (1 − 40/80) = 52.5 million.

3. Why do precedent transactions usually give higher values than trading comparables?

They include a control premium for gaining control and expected synergies.

4. In an LBO, name the three sources of equity value creation.

Operating improvement (EBITDA growth), debt paydown (deleveraging), and multiple expansion at exit.

5. An undeveloped oil field has a negative static NPV. Why can it still be worth a lot?

It is an option to develop later: if oil prices rise, the field becomes profitable; if not, you do not invest. High volatility and long time to expiry increase the option value.

6. A VC expects a CHF 300 million exit in 4 years and targets 40% per year. Post-money value today?

300 / 1.4⁴ ≈ CHF 78 million.

7. How should you value a bank, and why not with EV/EBITDA?

For banks, debt and deposits are operating raw material, so "enterprise value" and EBITDA are meaningless. Value equity directly with dividends or excess returns (ROE vs cost of equity), and use P/TBV vs ROTE.
