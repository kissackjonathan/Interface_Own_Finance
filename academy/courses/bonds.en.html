Part 1 · In briefWhat is a bond?

A bond is a loan that you can buy and sell. A government or a company (the issuer) borrows money from investors. In exchange it promises to pay regular interest — the coupon — and to repay the amount borrowed — the principal or face value — on a fixed date, the maturity.

A simple picture

You lend CHF 1,000 to the Swiss Confederation for 10 years at 1% per year. Every year you receive CHF 10. After 10 years you get your CHF 1,000 back. If you need your money earlier, you can sell the bond to another investor on the market — but the price you get depends on interest rates at that moment.

The one idea to remember

When interest rates go up, bond prices go down — and the other way round. If new bonds pay 3% and yours pays only 1%, nobody will pay full price for yours. Its price must fall until its total return (coupon + price gain until maturity) matches the 3% of new bonds. The longer the bond, the bigger this effect. This sensitivity is called duration.

Bonds matter for three reasons:

  • Size: the global bond market is larger than the global stock market (well over 100 trillion dollars).
  • Interest rates drive everything: government bond yields are the "risk-free rate" used to value every other asset, from stocks to real estate.
  • Portfolio role: bonds give regular income, are less volatile than stocks and — in many crises — rise when stocks fall.

A bond investor faces two main risks: interest rate risk (rates rise, prices fall) and credit risk (the issuer does not pay). Almost everything in this course is about measuring and managing these two.

Part 2 · HistoryFour centuries of lending

Key dates

  1. 1100s–1300sVenice, Genoa and Florence finance wars with forced loans to the city that can be traded between citizens — early government bonds.
  2. 1694The Bank of England is created to lend to the English government. A modern, trusted government debt market is born, helping Britain finance its rise.
  3. 1751Britain consolidates its debts into Consols, perpetual bonds paying 3% forever with no maturity.
  4. 1909John Moody publishes the first public ratings of railroad bonds. Poor's, Standard Statistics and Fitch follow. Credit ratings are born.
  5. 1938Frederick Macaulay defines duration: the average time it takes to receive a bond's cash flows.
  6. 1952Frank Redington, a British actuary, describes immunisation: matching the duration of assets and liabilities to protect an insurer against rate changes.
  7. 1971End of Bretton Woods. Currencies float; inflation rises. Bill Gross co-founds PIMCO and turns bond investing into an active, total-return business.
  8. 1974–77Robert Merton models a company's debt using option theory (the Merton model of credit risk). Oldřich Vašíček proposes the first major model of interest rate dynamics.
  9. 1979–81Paul Volcker, chair of the US Federal Reserve, raises rates to about 20% to kill inflation. The US 10-year yield peaks near 15.8% in September 1981 — the start of a 40-year bond bull market.
  10. 1980sMichael Milken (Drexel Burnham Lambert) builds the high-yield ("junk") bond market to finance riskier companies and leveraged buyouts.
  11. 1994The "Great Bond Massacre": the Fed raises rates unexpectedly fast and global bond markets suffer large losses. Orange County goes bankrupt.
  12. 1997The US Treasury launches TIPS, inflation-protected bonds (the UK had index-linked gilts since 1981).
  13. 1998Russia defaults on its domestic debt; LTCM collapses. Liquidity in "safe" bond trades disappears.
  14. 2008The Global Financial Crisis: mortgage-backed securities collapse, credit spreads explode, government bonds rally strongly.
  15. 2010–12The euro debt crisis (Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain, Italy). In July 2012 ECB president Mario Draghi says he will do "whatever it takes", and spreads collapse.
  16. 2015–22Negative rates: the Swiss National Bank sets its policy rate at −0.75% (January 2015 to 2022). Swiss government bonds trade at negative yields — investors pay to lend to the state. At the peak, over 18 trillion dollars of global bonds had negative yields.
  17. 2022The worst bond year in modern history: inflation forces central banks to raise rates fast. The US Aggregate bond index loses about 13%. In September, the UK gilt crisis nearly breaks pension funds using leveraged "liability-driven investment" strategies.
  18. 2023Silicon Valley Bank fails, partly because of large unrealised losses on long-duration bonds. In March, Credit Suisse's about CHF 16 billion of AT1 bonds are written down to zero in its rescue by UBS — a shock for bond investors everywhere.

What history teaches a PM

For 40 years (1981–2021) falling rates made bonds an easy, winning asset. Many investors forgot duration risk. 2022–23 reminded everyone that a "safe" government bond can lose 20–30% if it is long enough, and that "safe" bank debt can go to zero. Know exactly what risk you own.

3.1 The anatomy of a bond

3.2 Pricing a bond

A bond is worth the present value of its future payments:

Formula · Bond price \[ P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^T} \]

\(C\) = coupon per period, \(F\) = face value, \(T\) = number of periods, \(y\) = yield to maturity (the single discount rate that makes the sum equal to the market price).

Example

A 5-year bond with a 3% annual coupon, face value 100. Market yield for this risk: 4%.

The coupon (3%) is below the market yield (4%), so the bond trades below par (at a discount). The buyer earns the 3% coupon plus a price gain from 95.55 to 100, which together give 4% per year.

  • Coupon < yield → price < 100 (discount). Coupon = yield → price = 100 (par). Coupon > yield → price > 100 (premium).
  • As maturity approaches, the price moves towards 100 ("pull to par").
  • Clean vs dirty price: between coupon dates, the buyer pays the seller the interest earned so far — accrued interest. Quotes are "clean" (without it); you pay the "dirty" price = clean + accrued. Day-count conventions (30/360, Actual/365, Actual/Actual) define how accrued interest is computed.

3.3 Yields, spot rates and forward rates

  • Current yield = coupon / price. Simple but ignores the price gain or loss to maturity.
  • Yield to maturity (YTM): the internal rate of return if you hold to maturity, the issuer pays everything, and coupons are reinvested at the same yield.
  • Yield to worst: for callable bonds, the lowest of the yield to maturity and the yields to each possible call date.

Spot rates and the zero curve

Using one YTM for all cash flows is a simplification. In reality, each date has its own rate: the spot rate \(s_t\), the yield of a zero-coupon bond maturing at \(t\). The correct price is:

\[ P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_t}{(1+s_t)^t} \]

Spot rates are extracted from government bond prices step by step ("bootstrapping"): the 1-year bond gives \(s_1\); with \(s_1\) known, the 2-year bond gives \(s_2\); and so on.

Forward rates

Spot rates contain the market's implied future rates. The 1-year rate starting in 1 year, \(f_{1,1}\), satisfies:

\[ (1+s_2)^2 = (1+s_1)(1+f_{1,1}) \]
Example

\(s_1 = 2\%\), \(s_2 = 3\%\). Then \(f_{1,1} = 1.03^2/1.02 - 1 = 4.01\%\). Investing 2 years at 3% is equivalent to investing 1 year at 2% and then 1 year at about 4%. If you think rates in one year will be below 4%, the 2-year bond is the better deal.

Real yields and breakeven inflation

Inflation-linked bonds (US TIPS, UK linkers, Swiss and euro linkers) pay a real yield: their principal grows with inflation. Comparing them with normal bonds gives the market's inflation expectation:

\[ \text{Breakeven inflation} \approx y_{\text{nominal}} - y_{\text{real}} \]

If the 10-year nominal yield is 4.2% and the 10-year TIPS yield 1.9%, the market expects about 2.3% inflation per year (plus a small risk premium). If you expect more inflation, linkers are better; less, nominal bonds are better.

3.4 Duration and convexity: measuring rate risk

Macaulay duration

Formula · Macaulay duration \[ D_{\text{Mac}} = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{t=1}^{T} t \times \frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t} \]

The weighted average time (in years) until you receive the bond's cash flows, using their present values as weights. A zero-coupon bond's duration equals its maturity.

Modified duration: price sensitivity

Formulas · Modified duration and DV01 \[ D_{\text{mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1+y} \qquad\qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta y \qquad\qquad \text{DV01} = D_{\text{mod}} \times P \times 0.0001 \]

Modified duration tells you the % price change for a 1-point (100 basis points) change in yield. DV01 (dollar value of one basis point) is the money gained or lost when the yield moves 0.01% — the unit traders use to size positions.

Convexity

The price–yield relation is a curve, not a straight line. Duration is the slope; convexity is the curvature:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y + \frac{1}{2}\,C\,(\Delta y)^2 \qquad C = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{t(t+1)\,CF_t}{(1+y)^{t+2}} \]

Positive convexity is good for the holder: prices rise more when yields fall than they fall when yields rise.

Example (same 5-year 3% bond at 4% yield)

Macaulay duration = 4.71 years · modified duration = 4.53 · convexity = 25.6 · DV01 = 0.043 per 100 face.

Yield changeDuration onlyDuration + convexityTrue price change
+1% (to 5%)−4.53%−4.40%−4.40%
−1% (to 3%)+4.53%+4.66%+4.66%

For comparison, a 10-year zero-coupon bond at 3% (price 74.4) has a modified duration of about 9.7: a 1% rate rise costs almost 10%. A 30-year bond can lose 20% or more.

What increases duration? Longer maturity, lower coupon, lower yield. That is why, after years of near-zero rates, bond portfolios had record durations exactly when rates started rising in 2022.

Negative convexity

Callable bonds and mortgage-backed securities have negative convexity: when rates fall, the issuer repays early (or homeowners refinance), so the price cannot rise much; when rates rise, the bond's life extends and the price falls a lot. You lose on both sides — this is why such bonds pay a higher yield.

3.5 The yield curve

The yield curve plots government bond yields against maturity. Its shape tells you a lot about the economy:

  • Normal (upward sloping): long rates above short rates. Investors want extra return for lending longer.
  • Flat: often late in an economic cycle.
  • Inverted: short rates above long rates. The market expects the central bank to cut rates, usually because of a coming slowdown. In the US, an inverted 10-year/3-month curve has preceded most recessions since the 1960s — though with long and variable delays.

Theories of the curve

  • Expectations theory: long rates are the average of expected future short rates.
  • Liquidity preference / term premium: investors demand an extra premium for the risk of lending long. Long rate = expected short rates + term premium.
  • Market segmentation / preferred habitat: some investors need specific maturities (pension funds want long bonds, banks short ones), so supply and demand at each maturity matter. Central bank purchases (quantitative easing) work partly through this channel.

Curve strategies

  • Duration bet: longer duration if you expect rates to fall.
  • Steepener: buy short, sell long (DV01-neutral) if you expect the curve to steepen; flattener the opposite.
  • Roll-down: on an upward-sloping curve, a 5-year bond becomes a 4-year bond after a year and is priced at the lower 4-year yield — an extra price gain even if the curve does not move.

3.6 Credit risk

For non-government bonds, you must also ask: will I be paid?

The line between BBB− and BB+ is critical: many funds can only hold investment grade, so a downgrade to high yield ("fallen angel") forces sales and often an overshoot in price.

Credit spread and expected loss

Formulas · Credit spread and expected loss \[ \text{Spread} = y_{\text{corporate}} - y_{\text{government}} \qquad\qquad \text{EL} = \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD} \]

PD = probability of default, LGD = loss given default = 1 − recovery rate, EAD = exposure at default. A rough rule: spread ≈ PD × LGD + risk and liquidity premium.

Example

A BB bond with a 2% annual default probability and 40% expected recovery: expected loss = 2% × 60% = 1.2% per year. If its spread is 3%, investors are paid 1.8% per year above expected losses for bearing uncertainty and illiquidity. Historically, credit spreads have been larger than realised losses — the credit risk premium — but losses come in waves during recessions.

The Merton model

Merton (1974) saw that shareholders own a call option on the firm's assets with a strike equal to the debt: if assets are worth more than the debt, they keep the surplus; if not, they walk away and lenders take the assets. Lenders, therefore, have sold a put option. Consequences: credit risk rises with leverage and asset volatility; equity and credit markets carry information about each other (Moody's KMV uses this "distance to default").

Credit default swaps (CDS)

A CDS is insurance against default: the buyer pays an annual premium (the CDS spread) and is compensated if the issuer defaults. CDS spreads are a real-time market measure of credit risk.

Capital structure and bank capital

Bank bonds have special layers. AT1 bonds (Additional Tier 1, also called CoCos — contingent convertibles) pay high coupons but can be converted into shares or written down when the bank's capital falls or the regulator decides. In March 2023, about CHF 16 billion of Credit Suisse AT1 bonds were written down to zero while shareholders still received some value from UBS. Lesson: always read the bond's prospectus — the order of losses is not always what you expect.

3.7 Where bond returns come from

Formula · Approximate bond return over one year \[ R \approx \underbrace{y}_{\text{carry}} \; \underbrace{-\,D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y}_{\text{rate move}} \; + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}C(\Delta y)^2}_{\text{convexity}} \; + \text{roll-down} \; - \text{credit losses} \]
  • The starting yield is the best predictor of a bond portfolio's return over a horizon close to its duration. If you buy at 4% and hold for about the duration, your return will be close to 4% whatever rates do in between — higher reinvestment rates compensate for price losses. This is the core of immunisation.
  • Example: yield 4%, duration 6, rates rise 0.5% in a year → return ≈ 4% − 6 × 0.5% = 1%.

3.8 Managing a bond portfolio

Benchmarks

Common indices: Bloomberg Global Aggregate, Bloomberg US Aggregate, ICE BofA indices for high yield, and the SBI (Swiss Bond Index) family for CHF bonds. Be careful: bond indices are weighted by the amount of debt — the most indebted issuers get the biggest weight.

Sources of active return

  1. Duration: longer or shorter than the benchmark.
  2. Curve positioning: where on the curve (bullet, barbell, ladder).
  3. Sector and credit allocation: government vs corporate vs securitised, investment grade vs high yield.
  4. Issuer selection: credit analysis of individual borrowers.
  5. Currency and country: rates in different countries.
  6. Relative value: small pricing differences between similar bonds.

Bullet, barbell and ladder

  • Bullet: bonds concentrated around one maturity.
  • Barbell: short and long bonds, nothing in the middle. More convexity than a bullet of the same duration; benefits when the curve flattens.
  • Ladder: equal amounts maturing every year. Simple, steady reinvestment, popular with private investors.

Immunisation and liability-driven investing (LDI)

Redington's conditions for protecting a liability (for example pensions to pay) against small rate changes: (1) present value of assets = present value of liabilities, (2) duration of assets = duration of liabilities, (3) convexity of assets ≥ convexity of liabilities. Pension funds and insurers apply this with long bonds and interest rate swaps.

The 2022 UK gilt crisis

UK pension funds used leverage (repo and swaps) to increase duration cheaply. When gilt yields jumped after the September 2022 "mini-budget", they faced huge collateral calls and had to sell gilts, which pushed yields even higher — a doom loop. The Bank of England had to intervene. Leverage turned a hedging strategy into a source of systemic risk.

3.9 Bonds in a multi-asset portfolio

  • Diversification depends on inflation: when inflation is low and stable, growth shocks dominate and bonds rise when stocks fall (negative correlation, 2000–2020). When inflation is high and uncertain, rate shocks dominate and both fall together (1970s, 2022).
  • Currency hedging for a Swiss investor: unhedged foreign bonds add currency volatility larger than the bonds' own volatility, so bond allocations are usually hedged to CHF. The hedge return is roughly the short-rate difference: with CHF rates far below USD rates, hedging USD bonds to CHF removes most of their higher yield.
  • Liquidity: government bonds are very liquid; many corporate bonds trade rarely, and liquidity disappears in crises (March 2020).

3.10 A bond analyst's checklist

  1. What exactly do I own? Seniority, covenants, options, currency, prospectus details.
  2. Yield to maturity / to worst, and what part is compensation for rates vs credit.
  3. Duration, convexity, DV01 — how much do I lose if rates rise 1%?
  4. Where is this bond on the curve, and what do forwards imply?
  5. Credit: leverage, interest coverage, free cash flow, refinancing needs, rating trend.
  6. Is the spread enough for expected losses, liquidity and uncertainty?
  7. Inflation view vs breakeven.
  8. Currency: hedged or not, and at what cost?
  9. Role in the portfolio: income, diversification or return?

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. A bond has a modified duration of 7. Yields rise from 3% to 3.5%. Approximate price change?

−7 × 0.5% = −3.5% (slightly less in reality thanks to convexity).

2. Why does a zero-coupon bond have a higher duration than a coupon bond of the same maturity?

All its cash comes at maturity, while a coupon bond returns part of its value earlier. The weighted average time to receive cash is therefore longer.

3. The 1-year rate is 1% and the 2-year rate 2%. What 1-year rate does the market imply in one year?

\(1.02^2/1.01 - 1 \approx 3.0\%\).

4. A bond pays a 5% coupon and trades at 108. Is its yield above or below 5%?

Below. A premium price means the yield is lower than the coupon: the buyer will lose 8 points of price until maturity.

5. Why can a "safe" government bond lose 20% in a year?

Because of duration. A long bond with a duration of 20 loses about 20% if yields rise 1 percentage point. Credit safety does not mean price safety.

6. A company bond yields 6.5%, the government bond 4%. Default probability 1.5%, recovery 40%. What is the risk premium over expected loss?

Spread = 2.5%. Expected loss = 1.5% × 60% = 0.9%. Premium ≈ 1.6% per year.

7. Why did stocks and bonds fall together in 2022?

The main shock was inflation, which pushed rates up. Higher rates lower the value of both bonds (directly) and stocks (higher discount rate). Negative stock-bond correlation is typical only when inflation is low and stable.
