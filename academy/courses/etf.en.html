Part 1 · In briefA whole market in one share

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund that holds a basket of assets — often all the stocks of an index — and trades on the stock exchange like a single share. With one purchase you can own 500 US companies, 1,500 companies worldwide, all Swiss government bonds, or physical gold.

The one idea to remember

Most ETFs give you beta — the return of a market — at a very low cost (often 0.03–0.25% per year). Because most active managers do not beat their index after fees (see Investment Concepts), low-cost broad ETFs are the core of most well-built portfolios. The important choices are: which index, what it costs, how it replicates, and where the fund is domiciled.

Short history: the first ETF was launched in Toronto in 1990; the famous SPDR S&P 500 (SPY) followed in the US in 1993 and is still one of the most traded securities in the world. European ETFs started in 2000; iShares, Vanguard and others turned ETFs into a market of more than 10 trillion dollars.

Part 2 · How ETFs workThe few mechanics you must know

Creation and redemption

Large banks called authorised participants can create new ETF shares by delivering the underlying stocks to the fund, or redeem ETF shares for the stocks. If the ETF trades above the value of its holdings (its NAV), they create shares and sell them; if below, they buy and redeem. This arbitrage keeps the ETF price very close to the value of what it holds.

For Swiss investors: domicile and withholding tax

US companies withhold tax on dividends paid to foreign funds. An Irish-domiciled S&P 500 ETF suffers 15% US withholding at fund level, which you cannot reclaim. A US-domiciled ETF also has 15% withheld under the Swiss–US treaty, but a Swiss resident can usually reclaim it through the Swiss tax return. Swiss-domiciled ETFs on Swiss stocks avoid foreign withholding but Swiss withholding tax (35%) applies to distributions and is reclaimable by Swiss residents. Rules change and depend on your situation — check with your bank or a tax adviser.

Why leveraged ETFs decay

The index goes +10% then −10%: it ends at 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99 (−1%). A 2× daily ETF goes +20% then −20%: 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96 (−4%), not −2%. In choppy markets the loss grows with volatility. Leveraged ETFs are designed for one-day exposure.

Core and satellite

A common professional structure: a core (70–90%) of cheap, broad ETFs (world equities, home-market equities, high-quality bonds, maybe gold), and satellites (10–30%) for active ideas, factor tilts, sectors or single stocks. The core gives market returns at low cost; the satellites are where you try to add alpha.

Part 4 · The indicesThe 20 indices every investor must know

An index is a rule-based basket that represents a market. How it is weighted matters: market-cap weighted (bigger companies count more — most indices), price-weighted (higher-priced shares count more — Dow, Nikkei), or capped (no stock above a maximum weight — SMI). Also check if it is a price index (dividends excluded) or a total return/performance index (dividends reinvested — DAX, SPI).

Concentration in "diversified" indices

Market-cap weighting means a few giants can dominate: the largest ten stocks represent a large share of the S&P 500 and MSCI World, and three stocks make up around half of the SMI. Owning "the market" is not always as diversified as it sounds. Equal-weight or capped versions reduce this concentration.

Part 5 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. Does MSCI World include emerging markets?

No. For developed + emerging, use MSCI ACWI or FTSE All-World.

2. Why does the DAX look like it performs better than the CAC 40 over long periods?

Partly because the DAX is a performance index (dividends reinvested) while the CAC 40 is a price index.

3. You buy a USD-traded ETF on Japanese stocks. Which currency risk do you have?

Mainly the yen against the franc — the currency of the underlying assets — not the trading currency.

4. Why should you avoid holding a 3× leveraged ETF for a year?

Daily rebalancing causes volatility decay; over long periods, returns can differ hugely from 3× the index return, even losing money when the index rises.

5. What is the difference between the SMI and the SPI?

SMI: 20 largest Swiss stocks, capped, price index. SPI: almost the whole Swiss market, dividends reinvested.
