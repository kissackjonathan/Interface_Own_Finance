Part 1 · In briefEnergy: the base layer of the economy and of power

Every product, transport and data centre needs energy. Whoever produces, transports or controls energy has economic and political power — this is why energy is at the heart of geopolitics. The world's primary energy still comes mostly from fossil fuels: roughly 30% oil, a quarter coal and a quarter natural gas; the rest is hydro, nuclear (about 4% of primary energy, about 9% of electricity), wind, solar and biomass.

For investors, energy prices are among the most important macro variables: they drive inflation, trade balances, currencies of exporters and importers, corporate margins and the budgets of entire states. In 2026, the war with Iran and the near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz caused what the IEA called the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market.

The one idea to remember

Energy demand reacts very little to price in the short term (people still drive, factories still run), and supply takes years to build. So a small gap between supply and demand produces huge price moves. Prices are set at the margin by: spare capacity (mostly in the Gulf), inventories, the cost of the marginal barrel (US shale), and the risk of disruption at chokepoints. Geopolitics acts on all four.

  2. 1870–1911John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil controls about 90% of US refining; broken up by the Supreme Court in 1911 into companies that became Exxon, Mobil, Chevron…
  3. 1912Winston Churchill switches the Royal Navy from coal to oil: energy security becomes a matter of state.
  4. 1938Oil discovered at Dammam, Saudi Arabia. In 1945 Roosevelt and King Ibn Saud meet on the USS Quincy: US security in exchange for oil access.
  5. 1951–53Iran's prime minister Mossadegh nationalises Anglo-Iranian Oil (future BP); he is overthrown in a coup backed by the US and UK.
  6. 1960OPEC founded in Baghdad by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela.
  7. 1973Arab oil embargo after the Yom Kippur War: prices roughly quadruple. In 1974 consumer countries create the International Energy Agency (IEA) and strategic stocks.
  8. 1979Iranian revolution: second oil shock.
  9. 1986Saudi Arabia floods the market; prices collapse — contributes to the Soviet Union's economic crisis.
  10. 2008Brent reaches a record of about $147 in July, then collapses with the financial crisis.
  11. 2010sUS shale revolution (horizontal drilling + fracking): the US becomes the world's largest oil and gas producer. Prices crash in 2014–16 when Saudi Arabia fights for market share.
  12. 2016OPEC+ created: OPEC plus Russia and other producers coordinate output.
  13. 20 Apr 2020COVID: the WTI futures contract trades at about −$37 per barrel — storage was full.
  14. 2022Russia invades Ukraine: Europe loses most Russian pipeline gas, Nord Stream is sabotaged, G7 price cap on Russian oil, European gas hits records.
  15. 2026US–Israel war with Iran (from 28 February); Iran closes Hormuz (from 4 March); Iranian attacks halt Qatar's LNG and damage about 17% of its export capacity (3–5 years of repairs); IEA releases a record 400 million barrels; Brent above $120 at times; the UAE leaves OPEC and OPEC+ on 1 May after almost 60 years.

How the oil price is formed

  • Benchmarks: Brent (North Sea, global benchmark), WTI (US, Cushing), Dubai/Oman (Middle East crude to Asia). Physical cargoes are priced at differentials to these benchmarks.
  • Futures and the curve: backwardation (near contracts more expensive than later ones) = tight market, low inventories; contango = oversupply, storage pays. The curve shape is one of the best indicators of physical tightness.
  • Inventories: OECD commercial stocks and strategic reserves are the buffer; low stocks make prices jumpy.
  • Spare capacity: production that can be added within weeks — mostly Saudi Arabia and the UAE. It is the market's insurance. In 2026 the problem was not capacity but transport: spare barrels were trapped behind Hormuz.
  • Marginal cost: new US shale wells need roughly $60–65 a barrel to be profitable; when prices fall below, US output slows — a soft floor. Shale is "short-cycle": a well produces within months but declines quickly.
  • Fiscal breakeven: the oil price a state needs to balance its budget (Saudi Arabia roughly $90). It explains producers' behaviour.
  • Demand: global GDP, China, transport, petrochemicals, aviation; electric cars slowly erode gasoline demand.
  • Refining and products: crack spreads (product price − crude price); diesel tightness can drive crude.
  • Financial flows: speculative positioning (COT), CTAs, options; the dollar.
  • Geopolitical risk premium: see the Geopolitics & Markets course.
Formula · Why small gaps make big moves \[ \%\Delta P \approx \frac{\%\text{ supply gap}}{\varepsilon_D + \varepsilon_S} \]

With short-term demand elasticity \(\varepsilon_D \approx 0.05\) and supply elasticity \(\varepsilon_S \approx 0.05\), a supply loss of 2% of the market requires a price rise of about \(2\%/0.10 = 20\%\) to rebalance — before stocks are used. A 5% loss → about 50%. This is why stock releases, spare capacity and demand destruction matter so much.

OPEC and OPEC+: the cartel's power and limits

OPEC sets production quotas to stabilise prices; OPEC+ (since 2016) adds Russia, Kazakhstan, Mexico and others. Its power comes from spare capacity and discipline. Limits: cheating on quotas, US shale taking market share, members' different needs (Saudi Arabia wants stable high prices; the UAE wanted to use its new capacity). In 2026 the UAE left both groups from 1 May, and seven core members (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, Oman) paused increases in October–December after raising targets for six months. A capacity audit will set quotas from 2027.

3.2 Natural gas and LNG

Gas is harder to move than oil: by pipeline, or cooled to −162 °C as liquefied natural gas (LNG) on special ships. So gas markets are regional, with different prices: Henry Hub (US, cheap thanks to shale), TTF (Europe, Netherlands hub), JKM (Japan–Korea, Asian LNG). LNG links them: Europe and Asia compete for the same cargoes.

  • Largest reserves: Russia, Iran, Qatar (they share the giant North Field / South Pars), Turkmenistan, US.
  • Largest LNG exporters: the US (first since 2023), Qatar, Australia, Russia. Qatar's North Field expansion was set to raise capacity from 77 to 126 million tonnes a year by 2027 — delayed by the 2026 damage.
  • Europe's revolution: before 2022 Russia supplied about 40% of EU gas; Europe replaced it with LNG (mostly US), Norwegian gas and lower demand. Storage levels before winter are a key price driver.
  • 2026: Qatar halted production in early March; TTF jumped about 45–50% in a day; the forward curve rose for years ahead as traders priced 3–5 years of repairs.

3.3 Coal

Still the largest source of electricity in the world. China and India consume most of it (and produce most of it); Indonesia and Australia are the biggest exporters. Coal demand rises when gas is expensive (2022) — a reminder that the energy transition is not linear.

3.4 Nuclear energy

How it works, simply: uranium atoms split (fission) and release heat, which boils water; the steam turns a turbine. A plant runs 90%+ of the time, with no CO₂ emissions, but costs a lot to build and takes 10+ years.

What drives the uranium price

  • Most uranium is sold in long-term contracts between miners and utilities; the spot market is small and volatile.
  • Demand: reactors operating, under construction and planned; life extensions; restarts (e.g. Three Mile Island-1 for Microsoft); SMR first fuel loads; data centre demand.
  • Supply: mine disruptions (Kazakhstan's acid shortages, Niger), mine restarts at high prices, secondary supply (inventories, downblending).
  • Financial buyers: the Sprott Physical Uranium Trust buys spot uranium and can tighten the market.
  • Policy: sanctions on Russia, nuclear phase-outs or revivals (Japan restarts, Swiss and Belgian reversals, Sweden lifting its uranium mining ban).

SMRs and the AI link

Small modular reactors (roughly 50–300 MW) aim to be built in factories, cheaper and faster: GE-Hitachi BWRX-300 (Ontario, Canada), Rolls-Royce SMR (UK), NuScale and Westinghouse AP300 (US), plus Chinese and Russian designs. Big Tech (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta) signs nuclear power contracts to secure 24/7 carbon-free power for AI. Most SMRs are not yet commercial — execution risk is high.

Switzerland

Four reactors (Beznau 1 and 2, Gösgen, Leibstadt) produce about a third of Swiss electricity; Mühleberg closed in 2019. In 2026 parliament approved the government's counter-proposal to the "Stop Blackout" initiative, lifting the 2018 ban on new plants; opponents launched a referendum, so voters will decide.

Fusion: net energy gain achieved in a lab (US NIF, December 2022); ITER in Cadarache (France) and private start-ups — commercial power is still years or decades away.

3.5 The geopolitics of the energy transition

  • China dominates clean-tech supply chains: over 80% of solar panel manufacturing, most battery cells, rare-earth magnets, graphite (see the Rare Earths course). The dependence moves from oil wells to factories and minerals.
  • Electricity is the new oil: AI data centres, electric cars and heat pumps make grids, transformers and copper strategic.
  • Petro-states' dilemma: Gulf states diversify (Saudi Vision 2030, UAE's investments) while maximising oil revenues before demand peaks.
  • Energy security comeback: after 2022 and 2026, governments value reliable domestic supply (nuclear, renewables, storage, strategic stocks) more than lowest cost.

3.7 How to invest in energy

  • Integrated majors: ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell, TotalEnergies, BP — upstream + refining + trading; high dividends and buybacks.
  • National oil companies: Saudi Aramco, Petrobras, Equinor (partly state-owned), ADNOC subsidiaries.
  • Independents (E&P): ConocoPhillips, EOG, Diamondback — pure exposure to oil prices.
  • Services: SLB, Halliburton, Baker Hughes — leverage to drilling activity.
  • Midstream: pipelines and LNG terminals (Cheniere) — fee-based, more stable.
  • Refiners and tankers: profit from disruptions (cracks and freight rates jump).
  • Swiss angle: Geneva and Zug host the big commodity traders (Vitol, Trafigura, Gunvor, Glencore, Mercuria).
  • Nuclear: Cameco, Kazatomprom, physical uranium trusts, reactor builders, utilities with nuclear fleets.
  • Key metrics: breakeven oil price, reserve life, production growth, free cash flow yield at different oil prices, net debt, payout policy.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. Why did spare capacity not calm prices in 2026?

Most spare capacity is in the Gulf, behind the Strait of Hormuz: the barrels existed but could not be shipped.

2. What does a steep backwardation of the oil curve tell you?

The market is tight now: buyers pay a premium for immediate delivery and inventories are low.

3. Why are gas prices different in the US, Europe and Asia?

Gas is costly to transport; LNG requires liquefaction, shipping and regasification, so regional markets are linked but not equal.

4. Combined short-term elasticity of 0.1; supply loss of 3%. Approximate price rise?

3%/0.1 ≈ 30%.

5. Why is Western nuclear energy still exposed to Russia?

Russia's Rosatom has the largest enrichment capacity and supplies most of the HALEU needed by new reactor designs.
