Part 1 · In briefSmall markets, huge strategic power

Rare earth elements are 17 metals — the 15 lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium. Despite the name, most are not rare in the Earth's crust; what is rare is finding them concentrated enough to mine, and above all being able to separate them from each other, which is chemically very hard. They are essential for the strongest permanent magnets (in electric car motors, wind turbines, robots, drones, missiles and phones), catalysts, lasers and electronics.

Critical minerals is the broader family of raw materials that are economically essential and have risky supply: rare earths, lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, gallium, germanium, tungsten, antimony, copper, uranium and others.

The one idea to remember

The bottleneck is not mining but processing. China mines about 70% of the world's rare earths but refines about 90% and makes over 90% of the magnets. Prices in these small, opaque markets are driven less by geology than by policy: Chinese quotas and export controls, Western subsidies and price floors, stockpiling. To understand the price, follow Beijing and Washington, not just supply and demand curves.

A simple picture

Think of a restaurant where many farms grow the vegetables, but only one kitchen in the world knows how to cook the dish. Whoever controls the kitchen decides who eats and at what price. Opening a new kitchen takes 5–10 years, special skills and permits — and the existing kitchen can cut its prices to make your new one unprofitable.

Part 2 · HistoryFrom a Swedish quarry to a geopolitical weapon

  1. 1787A black mineral found at the Ytterby quarry near Stockholm leads to the discovery of several rare earths — four elements (yttrium, ytterbium, terbium, erbium) are named after the village.
  2. 1950s–80sThe Mountain Pass mine in California is the world's main source, driven by colour TV phosphors.
  3. 1982–84Masato Sagawa (Sumitomo) and, independently, General Motors invent the neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnet, the strongest permanent magnet — the foundation of modern electric motors.
  4. 1992Deng Xiaoping: "The Middle East has oil, China has rare earths." China builds its industry with low costs and lax environmental rules, and its exports flood the market.
  5. 2002Mountain Pass closes, unable to compete. The West becomes almost totally dependent on China.
  6. 2010After a fishing-boat incident near the disputed Senkaku Islands, China halts rare earth shipments to Japan. Prices jump more than tenfold by 2011. Japan responds by financing Lynas (Australia) and stockpiling.
  7. 2011–15Prices collapse as China resumes exports; Western projects fail. Molycorp, which had restarted Mountain Pass, goes bankrupt (2015). The WTO rules against Chinese export quotas (2014).
  8. 2017–20MP Materials buys Mountain Pass (2017) and lists in 2020; it initially sends its concentrate to China for processing.
  9. 2023–24China restricts exports of gallium and germanium (2023), bans export of rare earth separation technology (end-2023), and adds controls on graphite and antimony.
  10. 2025In April, China imposes export licences on seven heavy rare earths (including dysprosium and terbium) and magnets; Western carmakers face shortages. In July the US Department of Defense takes a 15% stake in MP Materials and guarantees a price floor of $110/kg for NdPr for ten years. In October China widens its controls; after the Trump–Xi meeting in Busan (30 October) it suspends the new measures for one year.
  11. 2026The US signs a similar price floor with Lynas (March). NdPr oxide peaks near $125/kg in February. In June, China puts MP Materials and USA Rare Earth on its export-control list. Supply chains outside China are being built — slowly.

Part 3 · The coreHow these markets really work

3.1 The elements that matter

Element(s)TypeMain useWhy it matters
Neodymium (Nd), Praseodymium (Pr) — "NdPr"LightNdFeB permanent magnetsThe largest value pool; the benchmark price for the industry
Dysprosium (Dy), Terbium (Tb)HeavyAdded to magnets so they keep their strength at high temperature (EV motors, defence)Very scarce; mostly from ionic clays in southern China and Myanmar; China controls almost all refining
Lanthanum (La), Cerium (Ce)LightCatalysts (oil refining, car exhaust), glass polishingAbundant and cheap; often in surplus
Yttrium (Y), Europium, Gadolinium, SamariumMostly heavyLasers, phosphors, medical imaging, samarium-cobalt magnets for defenceSmall markets, high strategic value

The problem of "basket" economics: a deposit contains a fixed mix of elements. A mine produces a lot of cheap lanthanum and cerium for every kilo of valuable NdPr. Revenue depends on the basket — this is why deposit quality (NdPr and Dy/Tb content) is everything.

3.2 The value chain and where China dominates

StepWhat happensChina's share (approx.)
1. MiningHard-rock ore (Bayan Obo in China, Mountain Pass, Mt Weld) or ionic clays (southern China, Myanmar)~70%
2. ConcentrationCrushing and flotation to a concentrateHigh
3. Separation / refiningSolvent extraction — hundreds of mixer-settler stages to split chemically similar elements into individual oxides~90% (almost 100% for heavy rare earths)
4. Metal and alloy makingReducing oxides to metals~90%+
5. Magnet manufacturingSintering NdFeB magnets~90%+
6. Motors and productsEVs, wind turbines, electronics, weaponsGlobal

Why China won: decades of state support, scale, cheap energy and labour, weak environmental rules for many years (separation produces toxic and sometimes radioactive waste — thorium and uranium), unmatched know-how and engineers, and willingness to accept low profits for strategic control. Consolidation into state champions (China Rare Earth Group, China Northern Rare Earth) gives Beijing tight control over output.

3.3 What drives prices up and down

Supply-side drivers

  • Chinese production quotas: Beijing sets annual mining and separation quotas. Higher quotas → lower prices (China used this to bankrupt foreign competitors in 2012–15); lower quotas or crackdowns on illegal mining → higher prices.
  • Export controls and licences: the most powerful lever since 2023. Licences can be slowed, denied to specific countries (Japan in 2010; US defence firms) or companies. Each escalation creates price spikes outside China and a two-tier market: prices outside China trade at a premium to Chinese domestic prices.
  • Myanmar: a major source of heavy rare earth ore for Chinese refiners; civil war, border closures and environmental damage frequently disrupt supply.
  • New projects: take 7–15 years from discovery to production (permits, metallurgy tests, financing, building a separation plant). Supply responds very slowly to high prices.
  • Government support outside China: price floors (MP Materials, Lynas), equity stakes, guaranteed purchases (offtake), stockpiles. They support Western producers but do not directly set the Chinese price.

Demand-side drivers

  • Electric vehicles: a typical EV uses 1–2 kg of NdFeB magnets. EV sales growth (especially in China) is the main demand driver.
  • Wind turbines: direct-drive offshore turbines use hundreds of kilos to tonnes of magnets.
  • Robotics and drones, data centres, consumer electronics, defence: growing, less price-sensitive demand.
  • Inventory cycles and panic buying: when controls are announced, manufacturers rush to secure stock, amplifying spikes; when fears ease, destocking pushes prices down.
  • Thrifting and substitution: high prices push engineers to use less dysprosium (grain-boundary diffusion), switch to ferrite magnets or induction motors, or design rare-earth-free motors. Each price spike reduces long-term demand intensity.
  • Recycling: still small, but growing with policy support.

Market structure: why prices are so volatile

  • No exchange and no futures market for most rare earths: prices are assessed by agencies (Shanghai Metals Market, Asian Metal, Fastmarkets, Benchmark) from reported deals. Opaque, thin, prone to jumps.
  • Small market size: a few billion to a few tens of billions of dollars depending on how you count — tiny compared with copper or oil — so one buyer, one policy or one fund can move prices.
  • Concentrated buyers and sellers: a few magnet makers, a few producers.

3.5 How to invest in the theme

  • Producers outside China: Lynas Rare Earths (Australia/Malaysia — the largest), MP Materials (US — mine, refinery and magnet plant), Iluka Resources (Australia — building a heavy rare earth refinery), Energy Fuels (US), Arafura (Australia), Neo Performance Materials (Canada/Estonia).
  • Chinese producers: China Northern Rare Earth, China Rare Earth Resources and Technology, JL Mag (magnets). Benefit from higher Chinese prices but carry policy risk.
  • Magnet makers outside China: Shin-Etsu, Proterial (Japan), Vacuumschmelze (Germany, private).
  • ETFs: VanEck Rare Earth and Strategic Metals (REMX), Global X Lithium & Battery Tech, Sprott critical minerals funds.
  • Diversified miners: Glencore (Swiss — cobalt, copper), Rio Tinto, BHP, Freeport (copper), Albemarle and SQM (lithium).
Risks specific to the theme
  • Policy whiplash: controls can be suspended overnight in a trade deal, deflating prices and stocks.
  • China's price weapon: China can flood the market to make Western projects unprofitable (as in 2012–15).
  • Execution and dilution: junior miners need years and repeated share issues; many never produce.
  • Metallurgy risk: a deposit is worthless if separation does not work economically.
  • Hype cycles: stocks often rise far ahead of real production, then fall 60–80%.
How a PM approaches critical minerals

(1) Map the chain: where is the bottleneck (often processing, not mining)? (2) Identify the policy lever and who controls it. (3) Look for companies with real production, government support (price floors, offtakes) and a low position on the cost curve. (4) Size small — these are volatile, event-driven positions. (5) Watch Chinese customs data, licensing news, Asian price assessments and Western subsidy announcements as catalysts.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. Why is processing a bigger bottleneck than mining for rare earths?

Separating chemically similar elements requires hundreds of solvent-extraction stages, rare know-how, permits for toxic/radioactive waste and years of work; China built nearly all of this capacity.

2. Why did MP Materials' $110/kg NdPr floor matter?

It guarantees a minimum price for the largest US producer regardless of Chinese price manipulation, making Western supply financeable — and it became a benchmark for other deals (Lynas).

3. What happened to lithium after 2022, and what is the lesson?

High prices triggered a wave of new supply and slower EV demand growth; prices fell more than 80%. Lesson: critical minerals are still commodities — high prices create their own cure.

4. Why are dysprosium and terbium especially strategic?

They keep magnets working at high temperatures (EV motors, defence), are scarce, and China controls almost all refining.

Part 5 · GlossaryKey terms

Critical mineral
Raw material essential to the economy with a risky supply.
Export control
Government licence requirement or ban on exporting a product.
Heavy rare earths
Dy, Tb, Y and others; scarcer and more valuable.
Ionic clay
Deposit type rich in heavy rare earths, easy to mine but environmentally damaging.
NdFeB magnet
Neodymium-iron-boron magnet, the strongest permanent magnet.
NdPr
Neodymium-praseodymium oxide, the benchmark rare earth product.
Offtake agreement
Long-term contract to buy a producer's output.
Price floor
Guaranteed minimum price, often paid by a government.
Solvent extraction
Chemical process used to separate rare earths.
Thrifting
Reducing the amount of a scarce material used in a product.
