Part 1 · In briefThe price of money

An interest rate is the price of money over time: what a borrower pays to use someone else's money, and what a saver earns for waiting. Because every investment is a trade between money today and money tomorrow, interest rates affect the price of every asset.

Two types of rates matter most:

  • Short-term rates are set (or strongly steered) by the central bank: the Swiss National Bank (SNB), the US Federal Reserve (Fed), the European Central Bank (ECB)…
  • Long-term rates (for example the 10-year government bond yield) are set by the market. They reflect expected future short rates, expected inflation and a risk premium.
The one idea to remember

The interest rate is the gravity of finance (an image often used by Warren Buffett). When rates rise, the value of future cash falls, so bonds, stocks — especially growth stocks — and real estate become worth less, and the currency tends to strengthen. When rates fall, the opposite happens. Central banks move rates to keep inflation under control and the economy stable.

Where we are (November 2026)

The SNB kept its policy rate at 0% on 24 September 2026, a level it has held since June 2025, and said it is ready to intervene in the currency market; its next decision is on 10 December 2026. In the US, the Fed raised its range to 3.75–4.00% in September 2026 — its first hike since 2023 — and held it in October. Always check the latest decisions on the central banks' websites: these numbers change.

Part 2 · HistoryHow central banks learned to control rates

  1. 1694The Bank of England is founded; it becomes the model of a central bank and the "lender of last resort" (Walter Bagehot, Lombard Street, 1873).
  2. 1898Knut Wicksell describes the natural rate of interest: if the bank rate is below it, prices rise; above it, prices fall. The ancestor of today's "neutral rate" \(r^*\).
  3. 1907The Swiss National Bank starts operating.
  4. 1913The United States creates the Federal Reserve.
  5. 1971President Nixon ends the convertibility of the dollar into gold. Money is no longer anchored to gold — central banks must anchor it themselves.
  6. 1979–82Paul Volcker's Fed raises rates to about 20% and breaks the inflation of the 1970s, at the cost of a deep recession. Central bank credibility becomes the key asset.
  7. 1990New Zealand adopts the first explicit inflation target. Most central banks follow. In 1993 John Taylor proposes the Taylor rule.
  8. 1999–2000The ECB starts with the euro; the SNB adopts its current framework: price stability defined as inflation between 0% and 2%.
  9. 2008–09Rates go to zero; the Fed (Bernanke), the Bank of England and later the ECB start quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds to push long rates down.
  10. 2011The SNB sets a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 CHF per euro to stop the franc's appreciation.
  11. 2015On 15 January the SNB removes the floor and cuts its rate to −0.75%. The franc jumps about 20% in minutes. Negative rates last until 2022.
  12. 2021LIBOR is replaced. In Switzerland SARON (Swiss Average Rate Overnight) becomes the reference rate.
  13. 2022–23Inflation surges after the pandemic and the energy shock. The fastest hiking cycle in decades: Fed to 5.25–5.50%, SNB to 1.75%.
  14. 2024–25Inflation falls; the SNB cuts six times, from 1.75% to 0% (June 2025).
  15. 2026The Fed, now chaired by Kevin Warsh, raises rates again in September as inflation stays above target; the SNB stays at 0%.

Part 3 · The coreHow rates work and how they move markets

3.1 The main rates

RateWhat it is
Policy rateThe rate the central bank targets (SNB policy rate, Fed funds range, ECB deposit rate).
Overnight reference ratesSARON (CHF), SOFR (USD), €STR (EUR): real overnight borrowing rates. Most loans and derivatives are priced on them.
Government bond yields2-, 5-, 10-, 30-year yields set by the market.
Mortgage and corporate ratesReference rate or government yield + a margin for credit risk.
Real rateNominal rate minus expected inflation — what really matters for investment decisions.

3.2 What central banks do

Mandates: the SNB must ensure price stability (inflation 0–2%) while taking the economy into account; the ECB targets 2% inflation; the Fed has a dual mandate: stable prices (2% inflation) and maximum employment.

Tools:

  • The policy rate, enforced by paying interest on banks' reserves and lending at a rate close to it.
  • Forward guidance: telling markets where rates are likely to go. Words move long rates without changing the policy rate.
  • Balance sheet: QE (buying bonds, which lowers long yields) and QT (shrinking the balance sheet, the opposite).
  • Foreign exchange intervention: a key SNB tool. A strong franc lowers import prices and hurts exporters, so the SNB sells francs (or buys them) to influence the exchange rate.

The transmission mechanism

Policy rate ↓ → money-market and loan rates ↓ → borrowing, investment and consumption ↑; asset prices ↑ (wealth effect); currency ↓ (exports ↑, import prices ↑) → demand ↑ → employment ↑ → inflation ↑. These effects take 12–24 months to reach inflation fully — Friedman's "long and variable lags". This is why central banks must act on forecasts.

The Taylor rule

Formula · Taylor rule (1993) \[ i = r^* + \pi + 0.5\,(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5\,(y - y^*) \]

\(i\) = suggested policy rate, \(r^*\) = neutral real rate, \(\pi\) = inflation, \(\pi^*\) = inflation target, \(y - y^*\) = output gap (GDP vs potential, in %). Example: \(r^* = 0.5\%\), inflation 3%, target 2%, output gap −1% → \(i = 0.5 + 3 + 0.5 - 0.5 = 3.5\%\). Central banks do not follow it mechanically, but it shows whether policy looks too tight or too loose.

The neutral rate \(r^*\) is the real rate that neither stimulates nor slows the economy. It cannot be observed, only estimated — one of the biggest debates in economics. Policy is "restrictive" when the real policy rate is above \(r^*\).

3.3 Real rates and inflation

\[ 1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + \pi^e) \quad\Rightarrow\quad i \approx r + \pi^e \]

A 4% nominal rate with 3% expected inflation is only a 1% real rate. Markets reveal expected inflation through breakeven inflation (nominal minus inflation-linked bond yield, see the Bonds course). Real rates are the true "cost of capital" and are one of the strongest drivers of gold, growth stocks and long-term valuations.

3.4 Reading the yield curve and market expectations

  • Long rate ≈ average of expected future short rates + term premium.
  • An inverted curve (2-year above 10-year) says the market expects cuts — often because a slowdown is coming.
  • A bull steepening (short rates fall faster) often happens when cuts begin; a bear steepening (long rates rise faster) can signal inflation or fiscal worries.
  • Futures tell you what the market expects: SARON futures for the SNB, fed funds futures for the Fed. The price of a 3-month futures contract is 100 − expected rate: a price of 99.80 implies about 0.20%. Sites like CME FedWatch convert these into probabilities for each meeting.
  • Markets move on surprises: a cut that was fully expected changes little; a hawkish message at the press conference can move markets more than the decision itself.

3.5 How rates move each asset class

Bonds

Directly, through duration: \(\Delta P/P \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y\).

Stocks

Higher rates raise the discount rate and (often) slow the economy. Growth stocks, whose cash flows are far in the future, are hit hardest — they have a long "equity duration".

Example with the Gordon model \(P = D/(r-g)\)
DividendGrowth gPrice at r = 8%Price at r = 9%Change
Value stock44%10080−20%
Growth stock26%10066.7−33%

The same 1-point rate rise hurts the growth stock much more. This is exactly what happened to tech stocks in 2022.

Sector effects: banks often benefit from higher rates (wider net interest margins) as long as credit losses stay low; utilities and real estate (bond-like, indebted) suffer; insurers earn more on their investments.

Real estate

Property value ≈ rent / cap rate. Cap rates follow long-term rates (with a lag). Higher rates also raise mortgage costs. In Switzerland, the SARON and the mortgage reference rate also drive rents.

Currencies

Formula · Covered interest rate parity \[ F = S \times \frac{1 + i_{\text{domestic}}}{1 + i_{\text{foreign}}} \]

With USD/CHF spot \(S = 0.80\), CHF rate 0.5% and USD rate 4% over one year: \(F = 0.80 \times 1.005/1.04 \approx 0.773\). The forward price of the dollar in francs is lower because dollars earn more interest. This is why hedging USD assets into CHF "costs" roughly the rate difference.

In practice, the currency with rising rates (relative to others) tends to strengthen in the short term. The carry trade borrows in a low-rate currency (often CHF or JPY) and invests in a high-rate one; it earns steadily and then loses heavily when risk aversion spikes and funding currencies jump.

Gold

Gold pays no interest, so its opportunity cost is the real rate. Historically gold has tended to rise when real rates fall — although since 2022, strong central bank buying has weakened this relationship.

How to follow central banks like a PM
  • Know the calendar: SNB quarterly (March, June, September, December), Fed and ECB about every six weeks.
  • Compare the decision and the statement with market pricing (futures) — the surprise is what moves prices.
  • Read the projections: SNB conditional inflation forecast, Fed "dot plot" and economic projections.
  • Watch the data the bank says it watches: core inflation, wages, employment, the exchange rate (SNB).
  • "Hawkish" = leaning towards higher rates; "dovish" = leaning towards lower rates.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. Nominal 10-year yield 3%, expected inflation 2.5%. What is the approximate real yield?

About 0.5%.

2. With r* = 1%, inflation 4%, target 2% and output gap 0, what does the Taylor rule suggest?

i = 1 + 4 + 0.5 × 2 + 0 = 6%.

3. Why are growth stocks more sensitive to rates than value stocks?

More of their value comes from cash flows far in the future, which are discounted more heavily when rates rise — a longer "equity duration".

4. The market fully expects a 0.25% cut and the central bank cuts by 0.25%. Why might bond yields rise anyway?

Because the cut was already priced. If the message suggests fewer cuts ahead than expected (a "hawkish cut"), long yields can rise.

5. Why do Swiss investors often pay to hedge US dollar assets?

Covered interest parity: the hedge return ≈ CHF rate − USD rate, which is negative when US rates are much higher.
