Part 1 · In briefThe institutions that write the rules of the world

International organisations are bodies created by states to cooperate: keep peace, lend money in crises, set trade rules, fight diseases, regulate banks, share data, protect culture. Some are universal (the UN with 193 member states), some regional (the EU, ASEAN), some are "clubs" of like-minded countries (G7, BRICS, OECD), some are cartels (OPEC) or military alliances (NATO).

For an investor they matter because they set the rules of the game: bank capital rules (Basel), trade tariffs and disputes (WTO), emergency loans to countries (IMF), sanctions (UN Security Council, EU), global minimum tax (OECD), oil supply (OPEC), emergency oil stocks (IEA). When these institutions weaken, the world becomes more uncertain — which is exactly what is happening in 2026, as the United States withdraws from dozens of bodies.

The one idea to remember

International organisations have only the power that states give them. They work when great powers find them useful (1945–2010s, under US leadership), and stall when great powers disagree (vetoes at the UN Security Council, the paralysed WTO appeals body, US withdrawals). Watch who funds, who votes and who vetoes — that tells you how much an organisation can really do.

Part 2 · HistoryFrom Westphalia to the 2026 retreat

  1. 1648Peace of Westphalia: the principle of sovereign states, equal and independent — the foundation of international relations.
  2. 1815Congress of Vienna: the "Concert of Europe"; the great powers recognise Swiss neutrality.
  3. 1863–65Henry Dunant founds the Red Cross in Geneva (1863); the first Geneva Convention (1864); the International Telegraph Union, today's ITU, is created (1865) — the oldest UN agency.
  4. 1874Universal Postal Union founded in Bern.
  5. 1919–20After the First World War, the League of Nations (in Geneva) and the International Labour Organization. The US never joins the League; it fails to stop the aggressions of the 1930s.
  6. 1930Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in Basel — the oldest international financial institution.
  7. 1944Bretton Woods conference: creation of the IMF and the World Bank.
  8. 1945United Nations founded in San Francisco (51 states); FAO; UNESCO (1945, in force 1946); 1947: GATT; 1948: WHO.
  9. 1949NATO: collective defence (Article 5).
  10. 1951–57European Coal and Steel Community, then the Treaty of Rome: the origin of the EU. 1960: EFTA, with Switzerland as a founding member.
  11. 1960–61OPEC (Baghdad) and the OECD (Paris, successor of the Marshall Plan's OEEC).
  12. 1967ASEAN founded by five Southeast Asian countries.
  13. 1975First summit of what becomes the G7, at Rambouillet.
  14. 1995World Trade Organization replaces GATT, with a binding dispute settlement system.
  15. 2001Shanghai Cooperation Organisation; China joins the WTO.
  16. 2002Switzerland joins the UN after a popular vote (54.6% yes).
  17. 2008–09Financial crisis: the G20 becomes the main leaders' forum; the Financial Stability Board is created in Basel; first BRIC summit (2009).
  18. 2015China-led Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank and the BRICS New Development Bank; Paris climate agreement.
  19. 2019The WTO's Appellate Body stops working after the US blocks new judges.
  20. 2024–25BRICS expand (Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE, Indonesia…). NATO's Hague summit (2025) sets a target of 5% of GDP for defence and related spending by 2035.
  21. 2026US exit from the WHO takes effect (22 January); on 7 January the US announces its withdrawal from 66 international organisations (31 UN entities, 35 others), after leaving UNESCO, the UN Human Rights Council and the Paris agreement. Part 3 · The coreWho does what — and why it matters for markets

3.1 The United Nations system

  • General Assembly: 193 states, one vote each; resolutions are not binding.
  • Security Council: 15 members, of which 5 permanent with a veto (US, China, Russia, UK, France). The only UN body that can impose binding sanctions and authorise force. The veto explains its paralysis on Ukraine, Syria or Gaza. Switzerland was a non-permanent member in 2023–24.
  • Secretariat: led by the Secretary-General (New York; large offices in Geneva, Vienna, Nairobi).
  • International Court of Justice (The Hague): disputes between states.

3.3 Clubs and forums: where great powers talk

  • G7: US, Japan, Germany, UK, France, Italy, Canada (+ EU). Coordinates sanctions (Russian oil price cap), aid to Ukraine, energy policy.
  • G20: G7 + China, India, Russia, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Indonesia, etc. + EU and African Union; about 80% of world GDP. Crisis coordination in 2008–09.
  • BRICS: Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, enlarged since 2024 to Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE and Indonesia (Saudi Arabia invited). A platform of the "Global South" seeking less dependence on the dollar — but members have very different interests (India and China are rivals).
  • SCO — Shanghai Cooperation Organisation: China, Russia, India, Pakistan, Central Asian states, Iran, Belarus; security and Eurasian cooperation.
  • World Economic Forum (Davos, Swiss foundation — not an international organisation): annual meeting of leaders and CEOs.

3.4 Security organisations

  • NATO (Brussels): 32 members; Article 5 = an attack on one is an attack on all. Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined after the invasion of Ukraine. 2025 target: 5% of GDP by 2035 (3.5% core defence + 1.5% related) — a structural driver of European defence stocks.
  • OSCE (Vienna): 57 states from Vancouver to Vladivostok; election monitoring, conflict prevention.
  • CSTO: Russia-led military alliance of some post-Soviet states.
  • Bilateral alliances: US–Japan, US–Korea, AUKUS (Australia–UK–US), the Quad (US, Japan, India, Australia).

3.6 Energy and commodity organisations

  • OPEC (Vienna) and OPEC+: see the Energy course. The UAE left both on 1 May 2026; Qatar left OPEC in 2019.
  • IEA — International Energy Agency (Paris): created in 1974 by consumer countries; coordinates emergency stocks (record 400 million barrels in March 2026), publishes the Oil Market Report.
  • Commodity study groups (coffee, cocoa, sugar, metals) — many now marginal; the US left some in 2026.

3.7 Courts and international law

  • ICJ (The Hague): disputes between states (e.g. genocide cases, maritime borders).
  • ICC — International Criminal Court (The Hague): prosecutes individuals for war crimes; the US, Russia, China and India are not members.
  • Permanent Court of Arbitration (The Hague): South China Sea ruling (2016), investor–state cases.
  • ICSID (World Bank): investor–state arbitration — key when a state expropriates foreign investors.

Switzerland in the system

Neutral since 1815, Switzerland is not in the EU or NATO but is a member of the UN (since 2002), the IMF and World Bank (since 1992, where it leads a constituency of Central Asian and Eastern European states), the OECD, EFTA, the WTO and the OSCE. It hosts the BIS and FSB in Basel, the UPU in Bern and dozens of organisations in Geneva, and plays a role of mediator ("good offices") and protecting power (it represented US interests in Iran for decades). This gives the country diplomatic influence and a large cluster of international jobs — but also exposure to funding cuts when big donors withdraw.

2026: a system under strain
  • The US, the largest funder, has left the WHO, UNESCO, the Human Rights Council, the Paris agreement and the UN climate convention, and announced the exit from 66 bodies. It keeps influence in standard-setting bodies where it competes with China (ITU, IMO, ILO).
  • The UN faces a cash crisis and cuts staff; agencies in Geneva reduce budgets.
  • China expands its own institutions (AIIB, NDB, Belt and Road) and its presence in UN agencies.
  • The WTO cannot settle disputes on appeal; trade becomes power-based rather than rule-based.
  • Investor implication: less predictable rules, more bilateral deals, higher geopolitical risk premium.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. Why can the UN Security Council fail to act on a war?

Each of the five permanent members can veto a resolution — including when it or an ally is involved.

2. What is the difference between the IMF and the World Bank?

The IMF lends short- to medium-term to countries with balance-of-payments crises and monitors economies; the World Bank finances long-term development projects.

3. Which institution in Basel sets bank capital standards?

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, hosted by the BIS.

4. Where are FAO and UNESCO headquartered?

FAO in Rome, UNESCO in Paris.

5. Why does a paralysed WTO matter for investors?

Without a functioning appeals body, trade disputes cannot be settled by rules, so tariff wars escalate more easily and trade policy becomes less predictable.

NATO's collective defence clause.

International bank capital and liquidity standards.
Conditionality
Policy conditions attached to IMF loans.
Good offices
Mediation and diplomatic services offered by a neutral state.

WTO principle: a trade advantage given to one member must be given to all.

Cooperation among many states through common rules and institutions.

The five permanent members of the UN Security Council.
SDR
Special Drawing Right, the IMF's reserve asset.

Autonomous UN organisation for a specific field (FAO, WHO…).

OECD global minimum corporate tax of 15%.
