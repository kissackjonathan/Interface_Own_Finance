Part 1 · In briefWhy gold still matters

Gold pays no interest, no dividend and has few industrial uses — yet central banks hold tens of thousands of tonnes of it, and its price rose from about $2,000 an ounce at the end of 2023 to a record of nearly $5,600 in January 2026. To understand gold, forget the idea that it is "just a commodity". Gold is a monetary asset: a form of money that no government can print and no counterparty can default on.

The one idea to remember

Almost all the gold ever mined still exists (about 216,000 tonnes), and new mining adds only about 1.5–2% per year. So the price is not set by mine supply but by who wants to hold the existing stock. The gold price moves when the desire to hold gold changes: when real interest rates fall, when trust in currencies or governments weakens, when central banks diversify reserves, or when fear rises.

A simple picture

Imagine a village where all the gold ever found is kept in people's houses. Each year a little more is dug up — a tiny amount compared with what is already there. If villagers suddenly worry that their paper money is losing value, they all want more gold; since almost none is being added, the only way to bring them gold is for current owners to sell — and they will only sell at a much higher price.

Part 2 · HistoryFive thousand years of money

  1. ~600 BCThe kingdom of Lydia (today's Turkey) strikes the first coins of gold and silver alloy.
  2. 1717As Master of the Mint, Isaac Newton sets a gold price for the British pound that de facto puts Britain on a gold standard.
  3. 1848–86California and later Witwatersrand (South Africa) gold rushes: big supply waves.
  4. 1870s–1914The classical gold standard: most major currencies convertible into gold at fixed rates. Long-run price stability, but deflation and rigid policy in crises.
  5. 1933–34US President Roosevelt bans private gold ownership (Executive Order 6102), then revalues gold from $20.67 to $35 per ounce — a 69% devaluation of the dollar.
  6. 1944Bretton Woods: the dollar is convertible into gold at $35 for central banks; other currencies are pegged to the dollar.
  7. 1968The London Gold Pool collapses: central banks can no longer hold the market price at $35.
  8. 15 Aug 1971President Nixon "closes the gold window". Gold floats freely; modern fiat money begins.
  9. 1980Gold spikes to $850 amid double-digit inflation and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, then falls for two decades as Volcker's high real rates restore confidence in the dollar.
  10. 1999–2002"Brown's bottom": the UK sells about 400 tonnes near the price low (~$275). The Washington Agreement limits central bank sales.
  11. 2000–08After a 1999 vote changing the constitution, the Swiss National Bank sells about 1,550 tonnes of its gold (it still holds 1,040 tonnes today).
  12. 2004The first major gold ETF (GLD) opens gold investing to everyone.
  13. 2011After the financial crisis and QE, gold reaches $1,921. In 2013 it crashes about 28% as real rates rise.
  14. 2014Swiss voters reject the "Save our Swiss gold" initiative (77% no).
  15. 2022Western sanctions freeze about half of Russia's foreign reserves. Emerging central banks conclude that dollar and euro reserves can be seized; their gold buying roughly doubles to above 1,000 tonnes a year.
  16. 2024–26Record after record: gold crosses $4,000 in October 2025 and reaches nearly $5,600 in January 2026, then falls sharply in March. Central banks bought about 863 tonnes in 2025, and gold ETFs had inflows of about 800 tonnes — the most since 2020.

Part 3 · The coreThe mechanics of the gold market

3.1 Supply: why it barely matters for the price

SourceTonnes / year (approx.)Comment
Mine production~3,600–3,700China, Russia, Australia, Canada, US, Ghana, Peru… Flat for a decade despite much higher prices: new mines take 10–20 years from discovery to production.
Recycling (scrap)~1,200–1,400The flexible part of supply: rises when prices jump (people sell jewellery), especially in India, the Middle East and China.
Total new supply~5,000About 2% of the above-ground stock (~216,000 t)

This stock-to-flow ratio (about 60 years of production already in vaults) is what makes gold different from oil or copper, where yearly production is consumed and stocks are small. A 5% change in mine supply has almost no effect on gold; a small change in the desire to hold existing gold has a large one.

Production costs: the industry's all-in sustaining cost (AISC) has risen to roughly $1,500–1,700 per ounce. Costs act as a very long-term floor, but with gold far above costs, mining economics do not drive today's price — they drive miners' profits.

3.3 What moves the price — the full list

1. Real interest rates (the classic driver)

Gold pays nothing, so its opportunity cost is the real (after-inflation) interest rate you give up. From about 2006 to 2021, gold moved closely and inversely with the US 10-year real yield (TIPS): when real yields fell, gold rose, and vice versa (2013 crash, 2020 surge).

\[ r_{\text{real}} \approx i_{\text{nominal}} - \pi^e \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{lower } r_{\text{real}} \Rightarrow \text{higher demand for gold} \]
The regime break of 2022

From 2022, US real yields rose sharply — and gold rose anyway. The real-rate model "broke" because a new, price-insensitive buyer dominated: emerging-market central banks, plus Chinese private demand. Lesson: price drivers can change. Always ask who is the marginal buyer today.

2. The US dollar

Gold is priced in dollars. A weaker dollar makes gold cheaper in other currencies and usually supports the price; a strong dollar weighs on it. For a Swiss investor, gold in CHF often moves less than gold in USD, because the franc also tends to rise in crises.

3. Inflation and currency debasement

Over very long periods gold roughly preserves purchasing power. But it is a poor short-term inflation hedge (it fell in the early 1980s with inflation still high). It reacts more to fear of future debasement — large deficits, rising public debt, pressure on central bank independence — than to monthly CPI numbers.

4. Central bank reserve policy and geopolitics

Gold is the only major reserve asset that is nobody else's liability and can be stored at home. After the freezing of Russia's reserves in 2022, many central banks decided to raise gold's share of reserves. War, sanctions and trade conflicts add to this structural demand.

5. Fear and crises

Gold often rises in geopolitical shocks and financial stress. But in an acute liquidity crisis (October 2008, March 2020) it can fall at first, because investors sell what they can to raise cash or meet margin calls.

6. Flows and positioning

  • ETF holdings (published daily) show Western investment demand.
  • COMEX futures positioning (CFTC COT report): when speculators are very long, the market is vulnerable to a sharp fall.
  • Trend-following funds (CTAs) buy breakouts and sell breakdowns, amplifying moves.
  • Options: heavy call buying can force dealers to buy futures (gamma), accelerating rallies.

7. Asian physical demand

China and India are the largest consumers. Watch the local premium or discount: when gold in Shanghai trades at a premium to London, Chinese demand is strong; a discount signals weakness. India's import duties and the rupee also matter. Seasonal patterns: Indian wedding season and Diwali (autumn), Chinese New Year (January–February).

8. Opportunity cost from other assets

Strong equity markets or high-yielding alternatives can pull money away from gold — and also bitcoin, which competes for the "non-government money" role among some investors.

3.4 How the market is organised

  • London (LBMA): the centre of the global over-the-counter market. Trading is "loco London" in 400-ounce "good delivery" bars (about 12.5 kg) stored in vaults (including the Bank of England's). The LBMA Gold Price is set by electronic auctions at 10:30 and 15:00 London time and is the global benchmark.
  • New York (COMEX): the largest futures market (100-ounce contracts), where most speculative trading and price discovery happen during US hours.
  • Shanghai Gold Exchange: the main physical exchange in China, with a yuan price; the Shanghai–London spread shows Chinese demand.
  • Switzerland: refines a large share of the world's gold (Valcambi, PAMP, Argor-Heraeus, Metalor in Ticino and Neuchâtel), recasting London bars into kilo bars for Asia. Swiss export statistics are a good indicator of where gold is flowing.
  • Futures pricing: futures trade above spot (contango) by roughly the interest rate minus the gold lease rate: \(F = S\,(1 + r - \ell)^T\). In early 2025, fear of US tariffs created a large premium in New York over London, and tonnes of gold were flown from London (often via Swiss refiners) to New York.

3.5 How to "value" an asset with no cash flows

Gold has no cash flows, so there is no DCF. Professionals use relative frameworks:

  • Real-rate model: regression of gold on real yields (and the dollar). The gap between model and price shows the size of non-rate demand (central banks).
  • Gold vs money supply: gold relative to M2 or to the Fed's balance sheet — a measure of how much paper money exists per ounce.
  • Share of reserves: what gold price would bring gold back to a given share of global central bank reserves.
  • Dow/gold and S&P/gold ratios: how many ounces buy the stock index; historical extremes (1980 near 1, 1999 above 40) mark long cycles.
  • Cost floor: industry AISC as a very long-run support.

3.6 Gold miners and royalty companies

Miners are a leveraged bet on gold because their costs are mostly fixed.

Example: operating leverage

Gold at $4,000, AISC $1,600 → margin $2,400 per ounce. If gold rises 10% to $4,400, the margin rises to $2,800: +16.7%. If gold falls 30%, the margin falls 50%. Miners therefore move roughly 1.5–3 times as much as gold — but they also carry country risk, cost inflation (energy, labour), operational accidents and the temptation to waste money on expensive acquisitions at the top of the cycle.

  • Majors: Newmont, Barrick, Agnico Eagle, Zijin (China), Gold Fields, AngloGold Ashanti.
  • Royalty and streaming companies (Franco-Nevada, Wheaton Precious Metals, Royal Gold): they finance mines in exchange for a share of future production at a low fixed cost — gold exposure with less operating risk.
  • Juniors: explorers; very high risk, lottery-like.

Gold in a portfolio

Many institutions hold 5–15% in gold as a diversifier: low long-term correlation with stocks and bonds, a hedge against currency debasement and extreme scenarios (stagflation, war, financial repression). It has long periods of poor returns (1980–2001: about −70% nominal) and is volatile (about 15% per year). Rebalancing keeps the position disciplined. Think of gold as insurance with a long-term positive expected return in fiat terms, not as a cash-flow investment.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. Why does a 10% fall in mine production have little effect on the gold price?

Mine supply is only about 2% of the existing stock each year; the price is set by the demand to hold the whole stock, not by new production.

2. From 2022 real yields rose sharply but gold went up. Why?

Price-insensitive central bank buying (after the freezing of Russia's reserves) and strong Asian private demand outweighed the higher opportunity cost of holding gold.

3. Gold at $3,000, a miner's AISC at $1,800. Gold rises 20%. What happens to the miner's margin?

From $1,200 to $1,800: +50%.

4. What does a large discount of Shanghai gold to London signal?

Weak Chinese physical demand (or import restrictions) — a bearish signal for the global price.

5. Why can gold fall at the start of a financial crash?

Investors sell liquid winners to raise cash and meet margin calls; gold often recovers once the liquidity panic eases.
