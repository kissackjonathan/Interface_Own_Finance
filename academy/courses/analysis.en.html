Part 1 · In briefThree ways to look at a market

Every investment decision tries to answer two questions: what to buy or sell, and when. Over the last century, investors have built three big families of tools to answer them:

  • Fundamental analysis studies the economic value of an asset: the economy, the industry, the company, its profits and cash flows. It asks: "What is this worth, and why?"
  • Technical analysis studies the market itself: prices, trends, volume and patterns on charts. It asks: "What are buyers and sellers actually doing, and where is the price likely to go next?"
  • Complementary methods study everything else that moves prices: investor sentiment, positioning and flows, relationships between markets, quantitative factors, events, options and alternative data. They ask: "What is already priced in, who is forced to buy or sell, and what am I missing?"
The one idea to remember

Fundamentals tell you what to own. Technicals help you decide when and how much. Sentiment and positioning tell you how much is already in the price. Each method alone has blind spots. Professionals combine them, and they are most confident when all three point in the same direction.

A simple picture

Think of buying a house. Fundamentals: the size, the location, the rent you could get, the interest rate on your mortgage. Technicals: are prices in this street rising or falling right now, are houses selling fast or staying on the market for months? Sentiment: is everyone at dinner parties talking about buying property (a sign of a top), or does nobody want to hear about it (often a good time to buy)? A wise buyer checks all three.

This course gives you each toolkit in depth, explains what the evidence says about which tools really work, and shows how to combine them into one disciplined process. (For the detailed valuation of a single company — DCF, ROIC, multiples — see the course Stock Analysis.)

Part 2 · HistoryFrom rice traders to algorithms

Key dates

  1. 1700sAt the Dojima rice exchange in Osaka, Japan, merchants trade rice futures. Munehisa Homma, a rice trader, records prices and studies traders' emotions — the origin of candlestick charts.
  2. 1884–1902Charles Dow publishes stock averages and writes editorials in the Wall Street Journal. His ideas, later called Dow Theory, introduce trends, phases and confirmation between indices.
  3. 1922William Peter Hamilton, Dow's successor, writes The Stock Market Barometer, formalising Dow Theory.
  4. 1932–34Richard Schabacker catalogues chart patterns. Two years later Graham and Dodd publish Security Analysis: the two schools — technical and fundamental — take their modern form at almost the same time.
  5. 1938Ralph Nelson Elliott publishes The Wave Principle: markets move in repeating waves driven by crowd psychology.
  6. 1948Robert Edwards and John Magee, Technical Analysis of Stock Trends — the classic reference on patterns (head and shoulders, triangles, support and resistance).
  7. 1950s–60sGeorge Lane develops the stochastic oscillator; Joseph Granville creates On-Balance Volume (1963); Richard Donchian builds systematic trend-following rules with price channels.
  8. 1970Eugene Fama formalises the Efficient Market Hypothesis. In its "weak form", past prices cannot predict future prices — a direct challenge to technical analysis.
  9. 1978J. Welles Wilder, New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems: RSI, ATR, ADX and the Parabolic SAR. Around the same time Gerald Appel creates the MACD.
  10. 1980sJohn Bollinger introduces Bollinger Bands. In 1983–84 Richard Dennis trains the "Turtle Traders" to follow simple breakout rules — many become very successful, showing trend following can be taught.
  11. 1986–91John Murphy publishes Technical Analysis of the Futures Markets and Intermarket Technical Analysis: bonds, stocks, commodities and currencies must be read together. Steve Nison brings Japanese candlesticks to the West (1991).
  12. 1992Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron find that simple moving-average and breakout rules had predictive power on the Dow over a century — before costs.
  13. 1993Jegadeesh and Titman document momentum in stocks: past winners keep winning for 3–12 months. Academic finance starts to accept a "technical" effect.
  14. 2000Lo, Mamaysky and Wang (MIT) test chart patterns with statistics and find that some carry modest information.
  15. 2012Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen publish "Time Series Momentum": trend following works across 58 futures markets over decades. Managed futures (CTAs) become a recognised asset class.
  16. 2010s–todayAlternative data (credit-card transactions, satellite images, web traffic), machine learning and options-market flows become standard inputs. The lines between fundamental, technical and quantitative analysis blur.

What history teaches a PM

For decades, fundamental and technical analysts looked down on each other. Today the evidence is clear: both contain real information, but much less than their fans claim. Fundamentals explain long-term returns; trend and momentum effects are among the most robust patterns in finance; many classic chart patterns add little after costs. The modern professional uses each tool for what it does best.

Part 3 · The coreThe three toolkits in depth

3.1 Fundamental analysis: top-down and bottom-up

Fundamental analysis can start from the big picture or from the single company:

  • Top-down: economy → asset class → country → sector → company. A macro investor first decides "growth is slowing and rates will fall", then buys long bonds and defensive sectors, then picks the best companies in them.
  • Bottom-up: start from individual companies with great business models and attractive valuations, almost regardless of the macro environment. Most stock-pickers work this way, but they still need to know which macro risks their portfolio carries.

Step 1: the economy and the cycle

The economy moves in cycles of expansion and contraction. Different assets and sectors do best in different phases. A classic map (it varies from cycle to cycle, so use it as a guide, not a law):

PhaseEconomyTypically strongTypically weak
Early recoveryGrowth picks up, rates low, central bank supportiveFinancials, consumer discretionary, industrials, small capsUtilities, staples
Mid-cycleSolid growth, profits risingTechnology, industrialsDefensive sectors lag
Late cycleInflation rises, central bank tightensEnergy, materials, commoditiesLong-duration growth stocks, bonds
RecessionActivity falls, rates cutConsumer staples, utilities, health care, government bondsCyclicals, credit

The key indicators to follow: GDP growth, purchasing managers' indices (PMI) (above 50 = expansion), inflation (CPI, core inflation), employment, central bank policy, the yield curve, credit spreads, and earnings revisions. Markets move on changes and surprises versus expectations, not on levels. A PMI rising from 45 to 48 is often better news for markets than a PMI falling from 58 to 55, even though 48 is "worse".

Step 2: the sector

Each industry has its own economics and its own key numbers. Knowing which metric matters is half of sector analysis:

SectorKey metricsMain drivers
BanksNet interest margin, CET1 capital ratio, cost/income, loan losses, P/B vs ROEInterest rates, credit cycle, regulation

SemiconductorsBook-to-bill, inventory days, capex cycle, utilisationStrong inventory cycles, technology shifts

PharmaPipeline (phase I–III), patent expiries, R&D productivityClinical trials, regulation, pricing
Real estate (REITs)Funds from operations (FFO), occupancy, cap rate, loan-to-valueInterest rates, rents, vacancies

Step 3: the company

Business model, competitive advantage, management, financial statements, earnings quality, balance sheet and valuation — all covered in detail in the Stock Analysis course. Two extra points matter for timing:

  • Earnings season: four times a year companies report results and give guidance (their own forecasts). The reaction depends on results versus consensus and, even more, on guidance.
  • Estimate revisions: when analysts raise forecasts across a sector, prices tend to follow for months. Revisions are a bridge between fundamental and quantitative analysis.

Fundamentals of other assets

  • Currencies: interest rate differentials, inflation (purchasing power parity), current account balances, safe-haven demand (the franc!) and central bank intervention.
  • Commodities: supply and demand, inventories, the cost curve (the price at which marginal producers lose money is often a floor), weather, geopolitics.
  • Bonds: inflation, central bank policy, government deficits, credit quality (see the Bonds and Interest Rates courses).
Limits of fundamental analysis
  • Timing: an undervalued asset can stay undervalued for years. "Markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent" (often attributed to Keynes).
  • Everyone has the same data: published numbers are instantly priced. The edge comes from better interpretation, longer horizon, or information others ignore.
  • Forecasting error: valuations depend on long-term assumptions that are very uncertain.

3.2 Technical analysis: reading the market's behaviour

Technical analysis rests on three ideas from Dow:

  1. Price discounts everything: all known information, hopes and fears are already in the price.
  2. Prices move in trends, which are more likely to continue than to reverse.
  3. History tends to repeat because human psychology (fear, greed, herding) does not change.

Why could it work? Information spreads slowly; investors under-react, then herd; large funds need weeks to build positions; many traders watch the same levels, which can make them self-fulfilling; forced sellers (margin calls, stop-losses, rebalancing) create predictable flows. Why might it not? In efficient markets past prices should not predict the future; many patterns are subjective and only look good in hindsight; after trading costs most simple rules lose their edge. The truth is in between — and the evidence (section 3.4) tells you which tools to trust.

Charts

  • Line chart: closing prices only. Clean, good for the big picture.
  • Bar chart (OHLC): open, high, low and close for each period.
  • Candlestick chart: the same information; the "body" is coloured green (close > open) or red (close < open), the "wicks" show the high and low. Easy to read buyer/seller pressure.
  • Timeframes: monthly and weekly charts for the main trend, daily for the intermediate trend, intraday for execution. Always start from the longer timeframe.
  • Log scale: on long charts, use a logarithmic scale so that equal % moves look equal (a move from 10 to 20 is the same as from 100 to 200).

Trend

An uptrend is a series of higher highs and higher lows; a downtrend, lower highs and lower lows. Anything else is a range. A trendline connects the lows of an uptrend (or highs of a downtrend); a parallel line gives a channel. Dow Theory describes three phases of a major trend: accumulation (informed investors buy quietly while news is bad), public participation (fundamentals improve, the crowd joins), and distribution (euphoria; informed investors sell to latecomers).

Support and resistance

Support is a price zone where buying has stopped falls before; resistance is where selling has stopped rises. They exist because investors remember prices: someone who bought at 100 and saw it fall to 80 is happy to "get out even" at 100, creating selling pressure there. Once a resistance is clearly broken, it often becomes support (role reversal). Round numbers, previous highs and lows, and gaps are common levels.

Moving averages

Formulas · Simple and exponential moving averages \[ \text{SMA}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} P_{t-i} \qquad\qquad \text{EMA}_t = \alpha\,P_t + (1-\alpha)\,\text{EMA}_{t-1}, \quad \alpha = \frac{2}{n+1} \]

The SMA gives the same weight to each of the last \(n\) prices. The EMA gives more weight to recent prices (for \(n = 20\), \(\alpha \approx 0.095\)), so it reacts faster.

  • The 200-day moving average is the most watched trend filter: price above it = long-term uptrend.
  • Golden cross: the 50-day average crosses above the 200-day (bullish). Death cross: the opposite.
  • Moving averages lag: they work well in trends and give many false signals in ranges ("whipsaws").

Momentum oscillators

Formula · Relative Strength Index (Wilder, 1978) \[ \text{RSI} = 100 - \frac{100}{1 + RS}, \qquad RS = \frac{\text{average gain over } n \text{ periods}}{\text{average loss over } n \text{ periods}} \]

Usually \(n = 14\). RSI moves between 0 and 100. Above 70 = "overbought", below 30 = "oversold". Example: average gain 1.2, average loss 0.8 → RS = 1.5 → RSI = 100 − 100/2.5 = 60.

Formulas · MACD and Stochastic \[ \text{MACD} = \text{EMA}_{12} - \text{EMA}_{26}, \qquad \text{Signal} = \text{EMA}_9(\text{MACD}), \qquad \text{Histogram} = \text{MACD} - \text{Signal} \] \[ \%K = 100 \times \frac{C_t - L_{14}}{H_{14} - L_{14}}, \qquad \%D = \text{SMA}_3(\%K) \]

MACD measures whether short-term momentum is stronger than long-term momentum. The stochastic shows where today's close sits within the recent high–low range.

  • In a strong trend, overbought can stay overbought for a long time — selling just because RSI > 70 in a powerful uptrend is a classic mistake.
  • Divergence: price makes a new high but RSI or MACD does not → momentum is fading, a warning (not a sell signal by itself).

Volatility indicators

Formulas · Bollinger Bands and ATR \[ \text{Bands} = \text{SMA}_{20} \pm 2\,\sigma_{20} \] \[ \text{TR}_t = \max\left(H_t - L_t,\; |H_t - C_{t-1}|,\; |L_t - C_{t-1}|\right), \qquad \text{ATR}_{14} = \text{average of TR over 14 periods} \]

Bollinger Bands widen when volatility rises and tighten when it falls. A squeeze (very narrow bands) often comes before a big move. The Average True Range measures typical daily movement in price units and is the standard tool to place stop-losses and size positions.

Volume

  • Volume confirms: a breakout on high volume is more reliable than one on low volume; a rally on falling volume is suspicious.
  • On-Balance Volume adds volume on up days and subtracts it on down days, to see if money is flowing in or out.
  • VWAP (volume-weighted average price): \(\text{VWAP} = \dfrac{\sum P_i V_i}{\sum V_i}\). Institutions use it as a benchmark for execution: buying below VWAP means you bought better than the average trader that day.

Chart patterns

Example: measured move

A stock forms a head and shoulders: head at 120, neckline at 100. When it closes below 100, the classic target is 100 − (120 − 100) = 80. Treat such targets as rough zones, not promises.

Candlestick patterns (doji, hammer, engulfing) describe one to three days of fight between buyers and sellers. They are useful to read emotion at key levels, but alone they have weak statistical power.

Fibonacci and Elliott waves

Fibonacci retracements (23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%) are levels where a pullback might stop. Elliott waves describe trends as 5 waves in the trend direction and 3 corrective waves. Both are very popular but highly subjective: two analysts often count waves differently. Use them, at most, as a way to locate possible zones — never as proof.

Relative strength and breadth

  • Relative strength (not RSI!): the price of a stock divided by its index. A rising line means it is outperforming. This is the chart version of the momentum factor and one of the most useful technical tools.
  • Market breadth: how many stocks participate in a move. The advance–decline line, the % of stocks above their 200-day average, and new highs vs new lows. An index rising while fewer and fewer stocks rise (narrow breadth) is a fragile rally.

Risk management: where technical analysis is most useful

Formulas · Position size and expectancy \[ \text{Shares} = \frac{\text{Capital} \times \text{Risk per trade}}{\text{Entry} - \text{Stop}} \qquad\qquad E = p \times \overline{W} - (1-p) \times \overline{L} \]

The stop is placed where your idea is proven wrong (below support, or e.g. 2 × ATR away). \(p\) = win rate, \(\overline{W}\) = average win, \(\overline{L}\) = average loss. Traders measure results in R (multiples of the initial risk).

Example

Capital CHF 50,000, risk 1% per trade = CHF 500. Entry at 100, ATR = 3, stop at 2 × ATR = 94 → risk per share 6 → buy 83 shares. If you win 40% of trades with an average win of 2.5R and lose 1R on the others: E = 0.4 × 2.5 − 0.6 × 1 = +0.4R per trade. You can be wrong most of the time and still make money, if losses are small and gains large.

3.3 Complementary methods

1. Quantitative and factor analysis

Score thousands of assets on value, quality, momentum, low volatility and estimate revisions, and check which factors your portfolio is exposed to (details in Stock Analysis and Investment Concepts). Momentum is the bridge between technical and quantitative analysis: it is a "technical" signal with decades of academic evidence.

2. Sentiment

Sentiment measures how optimistic or pessimistic investors are. It is mostly a contrarian tool at extremes: when everybody is bullish, there are few buyers left.

  • Surveys: AAII (US private investors), Investors Intelligence (newsletter writers), Bank of America Global Fund Manager Survey (cash levels, "most crowded trade").
  • VIX: the implied volatility of S&P 500 options, the "fear index". Very high VIX (above ~35–40) has often marked panic lows; very low VIX signals complacency.
  • Put/call ratio: many puts bought = fear.
  • Media and social media: magazine covers, retail trading volumes, IPO frenzy, "this time is different" narratives.
Sentiment is a condition, not a trigger

Extreme optimism can last for months while prices keep rising. Use sentiment to adjust risk and to look for a technical or fundamental catalyst — not to bet against the trend on its own.

3. Positioning and flows

Prices move when someone has to buy or sell. Who is already in the trade, and who will be forced out?

  • CFTC Commitments of Traders (COT): weekly positions of speculators and hedgers in US futures (currencies, commodities, bonds, indices). Extreme speculative positions often precede reversals.
  • Short interest and days to cover: a heavily shorted stock can rise violently on good news (short squeeze, e.g. GameStop in January 2021).
  • Fund flows: money entering or leaving ETFs and mutual funds.
  • Systematic flows: volatility-targeting funds, CTAs (trend followers) and risk-parity funds buy or sell mechanically when volatility and trends change. Pension rebalancing at month- and quarter-end.
  • Options dealers' gamma: when dealers are "long gamma" they sell rallies and buy dips, calming the market; when "short gamma" they must buy rallies and sell dips, amplifying moves.
  • Corporate buybacks: a large, steady buyer of shares, paused during blackout periods before results.
  • Index events: stocks added to a major index (S&P 500, SMI) are bought by index funds on a known date.

4. Intermarket analysis

Markets are connected. John Murphy's key relationships (which can change with the inflation regime):

  • Bonds and stocks: falling yields support stock valuations — unless they fall because of recession fears.
  • Dollar and commodities: commodities are priced in dollars; a weaker dollar usually helps commodities and emerging markets.
  • Credit leads equity: widening credit spreads often warn of equity weakness before stocks fall.
  • Copper/gold ratio: copper reflects growth, gold reflects fear; their ratio tends to move with long-term bond yields.
  • Cyclicals vs defensives, small vs large caps, banks vs utilities: ratios that tell you what the market thinks about growth.

5. Seasonality and calendar effects

Effects like the "January effect", "Sell in May and go away", turn-of-the-month strength or pre-holiday gains exist in historical data, but they are small, unstable and easily erased by costs. Treat them as background, never as a strategy on their own.

6. Event-driven analysis

Specific corporate events create predictable situations: earnings announcements, mergers (merger arbitrage: buy the target, sometimes short the acquirer, earn the spread if the deal closes), spin-offs (the new company is often sold by investors who do not want it), index inclusions, restructurings, share buyback announcements, activist investors.

7. Information from the options market

Formula · Expected move implied by options \[ \text{Expected move (1 standard deviation)} \approx P \times \sigma_{\text{implied}} \times \sqrt{\frac{\text{days}}{365}} \]

A stock at 100 with 30% implied volatility: expected 30-day move ≈ 100 × 0.30 × √(30/365) ≈ ±8.6. If you expect a bigger move around results than the options price, options may be cheap; if smaller, they may be expensive. Skew (puts more expensive than calls) shows how much investors pay for crash protection.

8. Alternative data and AI

Credit-card spending to estimate retail sales before results, satellite images of car parks and oil tanks, web traffic and app downloads, job postings, shipping data, and natural language processing of earnings-call transcripts (is the CEO's tone more or less confident than last quarter?). These help forecast fundamentals before they are published — but the data is expensive, noisy, and the edge disappears as more funds use it.

How to test any method honestly
  1. Write the rule precisely before looking at the results.
  2. Test on many assets and periods, including out-of-sample data you did not use to design it.
  3. Include realistic costs, slippage and taxes.
  4. Compare with a simple benchmark (buy and hold, a 200-day moving-average rule).
  5. Be suspicious of anything that only works with very specific parameters (e.g. a 37-day average but not 35 or 40).

3.5 Combining the methods: one process

Professionals combine the toolkits in a structured way. A practical framework:

  1. Macro regime (fundamental, top-down): growth up or down? inflation up or down? central banks easing or tightening? → decides overall risk level and asset class tilts.
  2. Idea generation (fundamental + quant screens): cheap, high-quality assets with improving fundamentals or positive revisions.
  3. Deep fundamental work: thesis, valuation range, catalysts, risks.
  4. Market check (technical + sentiment + positioning): is the trend confirming or fighting the thesis? Is the trade crowded? What is priced in?
  5. Entry and sizing (technical + risk): enter near support or on a confirmed breakout, stop where the thesis is wrong, size with ATR and risk budget.
  6. Monitoring: fundamental milestones, technical levels, changes in positioning. Exit when the thesis breaks, the target is reached, or the stop is hit — not because of emotion.
Example: a scoring grid
DimensionSignalScore
MacroEarly recovery, rates falling — supportive for the sector+1
ValuationEV/EBIT 20% below 10-year average, FCF yield 6%+1

If valuation and quality are strong but the trend is down and revisions negative, a professional typically starts small or waits: the market may know something you do not yet see.

Rules professionals live by
  • Do not fight a strong trend with only a valuation argument — wait for a catalyst or a change in trend.
  • Do not buy a strong chart with terrible fundamentals and big size — momentum can reverse in a day.
  • When fundamentals and price disagree, ask what the market knows. Then decide who is wrong.
  • Define the exit before the entry.
  • Keep a journal: why you entered, what each method said, what happened. Review it to see which tools really help you.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. A stock is 30% below your fair value estimate, but it is below its 200-day average, making lower lows, and analysts keep cutting estimates. What do you do?

Usually wait or start very small. The trend and revisions say the market sees deteriorating fundamentals. Look for a catalyst or a change in trend (higher low, break of resistance, revisions stabilising) before building a full position.

2. Average gain over 14 days is 2 and average loss is 1. What is the RSI?

RS = 2. RSI = 100 − 100/3 ≈ 66.7.

3. Why is a breakout on low volume less reliable?

It shows little real participation: few buyers are committing money at the new level, so the move can easily fail.

4. Your capital is CHF 20,000, you risk 1% per trade, entry 50, stop 47. How many shares?

Risk = CHF 200; risk per share = 3 → 66 shares.

5. The S&P 500 makes a new high, but only 40% of its stocks are above their 200-day average. What does this suggest?

Narrow breadth: the rally is carried by a few large stocks. Such markets are more fragile and vulnerable if the leaders stumble.

6. The VIX is at 45 and the fund manager survey shows record cash levels. Is it a buy signal?

It is a contrarian condition (extreme fear), historically often near lows. But wait for confirmation — stabilising prices, improving breadth or a policy response — and size carefully.

7. Which technical effects have the strongest academic evidence?

Cross-sectional momentum and time-series momentum (trend following), plus post-earnings drift and revisions momentum. Most classic chart patterns have much weaker evidence.
