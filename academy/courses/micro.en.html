Part 1 · In briefHow markets and companies really behave

Microeconomics studies the decisions of individual consumers and firms, and how they meet in markets to set prices and quantities. For an investor, micro is the science behind three questions that decide a company's profits:

  • Demand: how many people want the product, and how much does demand fall if the price rises?
  • Costs: how do costs change with volume — does the company get cheaper as it grows?
  • Competition: how many rivals are there, can new ones enter, and how do they react to each other?
The one idea to remember

In a competitive market, high profits attract competitors until profits fall back to the cost of capital. A company keeps high margins only if something limits competition: scale, network effects, brands, patents, switching costs, regulation. Micro tells you whether a company has pricing power — which Warren Buffett has called the single most important factor in judging a business.

A simple picture

One ice-cream seller on a hot beach can charge CHF 6 per cone. When ten sellers arrive, the price falls towards the cost of making a cone. If one of them has a famous brand, the only freezer on the beach, or a contract with the beach owner, it can keep charging more. Investing is largely about finding the seller with the protected spot.

Part 3 · The coreThe tools of microeconomics, applied to companies

3.1 Supply, demand and elasticity

The demand curve slopes down: the higher the price, the fewer units buyers want. The supply curve slopes up: higher prices make producers supply more. The market price is where they cross (equilibrium). Shifts in either curve (a new technology, a change in tastes, a drought) move the price.

Formula · Price elasticity of demand \[ E_d = \frac{\%\,\Delta Q}{\%\,\Delta P} \]

If \(|E_d| < 1\), demand is inelastic: raising the price increases revenue (necessities, addictive products, essential software, medicines). If \(|E_d| > 1\), demand is elastic: raising the price reduces revenue (commodity-like products with many substitutes).

Example

A software company raises prices 10% and loses 3% of customers: \(E_d = -0.3\). Revenue ≈ 1.10 × 0.97 = +6.7%, and since costs barely change, profits rise much more. A commodity chemical producer that raises prices 10% may lose 25% of volume to competitors: \(E_d = -2.5\), revenue falls about 17.5%.

Other elasticities: income elasticity (luxury goods > 1: demand rises faster than income; staples < 1), cross-price elasticity (substitutes > 0, complements < 0).

3.2 Costs and scale

  • Fixed costs (rent, R&D, software development) do not change with volume; variable costs (materials, delivery) do.
  • Marginal cost: the cost of one more unit. For software and digital products it is close to zero — the root of their very high margins at scale.
  • Economies of scale: average cost falls as volume rises (spreading fixed costs, buying power). Economies of scope: producing several products together is cheaper. Learning curve: costs fall with cumulative experience (solar panels, batteries: each doubling of cumulative production has cut costs by roughly 20%).
Formula · Break-even volume \[ Q^* = \frac{\text{Fixed costs}}{P - VC} \]

\(P - VC\) is the contribution margin per unit. High fixed costs and high contribution margins mean strong operating leverage: profits explode above break-even and collapse below it (airlines, semiconductors, hotels).

Formulas · Measuring market power \[ \text{Lerner index} = \frac{P - MC}{P} = \frac{1}{|E_d|} \qquad\qquad \text{HHI} = \sum_{i} s_i^2 \]

The Lerner index measures the markup over marginal cost; a profit-maximising firm sets a higher markup when demand is less elastic. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index sums the squared market shares (in %). Example: five firms with 30, 25, 20, 15 and 10% → HHI = 900 + 625 + 400 + 225 + 100 = 2,250. US merger guidelines (2023) consider a market above 1,800 highly concentrated.

3.4 Pricing power and pricing strategies

  • Price discrimination: charging different customers different prices according to willingness to pay — airline fares, student discounts, software editions (basic / pro / enterprise).
  • Bundling: selling products together (software suites, telecom packages) to capture more value and raise switching costs.
  • Subscriptions and recurring revenue: predictable cash flows, high lifetime value, easier price increases.
  • Razor-and-blade: cheap device, profitable consumables (printers, coffee capsules, medical devices).
  • Value-based pricing: price set on the value created for the client, not on cost — the hallmark of strong businesses.
Testing pricing power

Look at: gross margin level and stability through inflation (2022–23 was a perfect test), history of price increases versus volume lost, the share of the client's total cost (a small but critical input is easy to raise in price), and how customers react when competitors cut prices.

3.5 Game theory: competition as strategy

In an oligopoly, each firm's best decision depends on what the others do. Game theory models this.

Example: the price war (prisoner's dilemma)

Two airlines can keep prices high or cut. Profits (Airline A, Airline B):

B keeps highB cuts
A keeps high(100, 100)(20, 130)
A cuts(130, 20)(40, 40)

Whatever B does, A earns more by cutting (130 > 100 and 40 > 20), and the same for B. The Nash equilibrium is both cutting (40, 40), although both keeping prices high (100, 100) would be better for both. This is why industries with many similar competitors and high fixed costs often have price wars.

  • Repeated games: when firms meet again and again, they can sustain high prices through tacit cooperation ("if you cut, I will punish you"). Stable oligopolies with few players, transparent prices and patient owners tend to be profitable.
  • Entry deterrence: incumbents build excess capacity, cut prices or lock in customers to discourage newcomers.
  • First-mover advantage: valuable when there are network effects, scale or switching costs; otherwise, followers often win by learning from the pioneer's mistakes.

3.6 Network effects and platforms

A product has network effects when each new user makes it more valuable for the others (phones, social networks, payment systems, marketplaces). Metcalfe's law suggests the value of a network grows roughly with the square of its users (\(n^2\)), though in practice growth is slower.

  • Two-sided markets: platforms connect two groups (buyers and sellers, drivers and riders, card holders and merchants). They often subsidise one side to attract the other (the "chicken-and-egg" problem).
  • Winner-take-most: strong network effects lead to one or two dominant players with high margins and durable moats.
  • Limits: multi-homing (users on several platforms), local networks, and regulation can weaken them.

3.7 Information and incentives

  • Adverse selection (Akerlof's "lemons"): when sellers know more than buyers, bad products drive out good ones. Insurers, lenders and used-car markets fight it with screening, warranties and reputation.
  • Moral hazard: being protected from a risk changes behaviour (insured drivers take more risk; "too big to fail" banks take more risk).
  • Principal–agent problem: managers (agents) may not act in shareholders' (principals') interest — empire building, short-termism. Hence the importance of incentive design: what are managers paid on?
  • Signalling: dividends, buybacks and insider purchases can signal management's confidence.

3.8 Applying micro to an investment

  1. What is the market structure? How concentrated (HHI)? Is it changing?
  2. How elastic is demand? Has the company raised prices without losing volume?
  3. What is the cost structure? Fixed vs variable, scale economies, learning curve, marginal cost.
  4. Is there a network effect, switching costs or other barrier to entry?
  5. How do competitors behave: rational oligopoly or recurrent price wars?
  6. Are management incentives aligned with long-term value?
  7. Could regulation (antitrust, price controls) cap profits?

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. A company raises prices 8% and volume falls 2%. Is demand elastic or inelastic? What happens to revenue?

Elasticity = −2/8 = −0.25: inelastic. Revenue ≈ 1.08 × 0.98 − 1 ≈ +5.8%.

2. Fixed costs are 2 million, price 50, variable cost 30. Break-even volume?

2,000,000 / 20 = 100,000 units.

3. Four firms each have 25% of a market. What is the HHI?

4 × 625 = 2,500 — highly concentrated.

4. Why do airlines often have price wars even though they would all be better off with high prices?

Prisoner's dilemma: each airline gains by cutting whatever the others do; high fixed costs and perishable seats make filling planes at low prices tempting.

5. Why can software firms have very high margins at scale?

Very high fixed development costs but near-zero marginal cost: each extra customer adds revenue with almost no extra cost.

Part 5 · GlossaryKey terms

Adverse selection
Bad risks or products drive out good ones when information is asymmetric.
Barrier to entry
Anything that makes it hard for new competitors to enter.
Elasticity
Sensitivity of quantity to a change in price or income.
Economies of scale
Falling average cost as volume rises.
HHI
Sum of squared market shares; measure of concentration.
Lerner index
Markup over marginal cost as a share of price.
Marginal cost
Cost of producing one more unit.
Moral hazard
More risk-taking when protected from consequences.
Nash equilibrium
Situation where no player gains by changing strategy alone.
Network effect
A product becomes more valuable as more people use it.
Oligopoly
Market dominated by a few interdependent firms.
Pricing power
Ability to raise prices without losing much volume.
