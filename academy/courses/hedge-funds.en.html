Part 1 · In briefWhat is a hedge fund?

A hedge fund is a private investment fund, open only to professional or wealthy investors, that can use tools most traditional funds cannot: short selling (betting that prices will fall), leverage (borrowed money), derivatives and concentrated or illiquid positions. Its goal is usually absolute return — making money whatever markets do — rather than beating an index.

Hedge funds charge high fees, classically "2 and 20": a 2% annual management fee on assets plus 20% of profits. The industry manages a record amount of capital: according to HFR, $5.64 trillion at the end of September 2026, the 16th consecutive quarterly increase.

The one idea to remember

A hedge fund sells alpha — returns that do not come from simply owning the market — and diversification. Investors should only pay hedge fund fees for returns they cannot get cheaply elsewhere. Many funds mainly deliver hidden beta (market exposure) or factor exposure at a high price; the best deliver real skill, risk control and low correlation with stocks and bonds.

  1. 1949Alfred Winslow Jones, a sociologist and journalist, creates the first hedge fund: he buys stocks he likes, shorts stocks he dislikes to "hedge" market risk, uses leverage, and takes 20% of profits.
  2. 1966A Fortune article reveals Jones beat the best mutual funds; dozens of hedge funds launch.
  3. 1973George Soros and Jim Rogers found the fund that will become Quantum. Global macro is born.
  4. 1975–80Ray Dalio founds Bridgewater (1975); Julian Robertson founds Tiger (1980); Paul Tudor Jones founds Tudor (1980).
  5. 1982–88Jim Simons founds Renaissance Technologies; the Medallion fund starts in 1988 and becomes the best-performing fund in history.
  6. 1989–90Izzy Englander founds Millennium (1989); Ken Griffin founds Citadel (1990) — the future multi-manager giants.
  7. 16 Sep 1992"Black Wednesday": Soros and Stanley Druckenmiller bet against the British pound, forcing it out of the European exchange rate mechanism; Quantum earns about $1 billion.
  8. 1998Long-Term Capital Management, run by bond-arbitrage stars and two Nobel laureates, loses most of its capital after the Russian default; the Fed organises a $3.6 billion private rescue to avoid systemic damage.
  9. 2000Robertson closes Tiger after missing the tech bubble; his protégés ("Tiger cubs": Viking, Lone Pine, Maverick, Tiger Global, Coatue) go on to dominate long/short equity.
  10. 2007John Paulson bets against subprime mortgages — his firm makes about $15 billion. The August 2007 "quant quake" hits crowded quantitative funds.
  11. 2008Industry losses of about 19%; funds impose gates on redemptions. In December, Bernard Madoff's $65 billion fraud is revealed — operational due diligence becomes essential.
  12. 2010sWeak average returns and fee pressure; rise of multi-manager platforms and of liquid alternatives; activist investors (Ackman, Icahn, Elliott) become powerful.
  13. 2021GameStop: retail traders squeeze short sellers; Melvin Capital loses heavily. Archegos, a family office using hidden leverage through total return swaps, collapses; banks lose over $10 billion (Credit Suisse about $5.5 billion).
  14. 2022–26Macro funds shine as rates and inflation return; multi-strategy platforms grow to a record $900 billion (HFR, Q3 2026), and capital concentrates in the largest firms.

Part 3 · The coreHow hedge funds make (and lose) money

3.1 Structure and economics

  • Legal form: usually a limited partnership or offshore company (Cayman Islands); the manager is the general partner. In Switzerland, hedge fund strategies are accessed mainly through banks, funds of funds and Luxembourg/Irish vehicles; Geneva and Zug host many managers.
  • Investors: pension funds, endowments, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, family offices, private banks.
  • Service providers: prime brokers (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan…) lend money and securities, hold assets and execute trades; administrators calculate the NAV; auditors check.
  • Liquidity terms: monthly or quarterly redemptions with 30–90 days' notice, lock-ups (1–3 years), gates (limit on withdrawals per period), side pockets for illiquid assets.

Fees

How fees work

Management fee = 2% × assets. Performance fee = 20% × profits above the high-water mark (the previous peak NAV — the manager is not paid twice for recovering losses), sometimes only above a hurdle (e.g. the cash rate). Many multi-manager platforms use pass-through fees: instead of a fixed management fee, they charge investors all operating costs (trader pay, data, technology), which can be large, plus a performance fee.

Example

A fund of 100 earns 15% gross → 115. Management fee 2 → 113. Profit 13; performance fee 20% × 13 = 2.6 → net 110.4. The investor keeps 10.4% of the 15% gross: fees took about a third of the return. If the fund then loses 10%, no performance fee is due until it gets back above its high-water mark.

Exposure: gross, net and beta-adjusted

Formulas · Exposure \[ \text{Gross} = \frac{\text{Longs} + |\text{Shorts}|}{\text{Capital}} \qquad \text{Net} = \frac{\text{Longs} - |\text{Shorts}|}{\text{Capital}} \qquad \text{Beta-adj. net} = \frac{\sum \beta_i L_i - \sum \beta_j |S_j|}{\text{Capital}} \]

Example: capital 100, longs 150 (average beta 1.1), shorts 100 (average beta 1.3). Gross = 250%, net = 50%, beta-adjusted net = (165 − 130)/100 = 35%. Gross measures how much is at work (and leverage risk); net and beta-adjusted net measure market direction risk.

3.2 The main strategies — mechanics and what drives their returns

1. Equity long/short

Buy undervalued stocks, short overvalued ones. Return = market exposure (net) + stock-picking alpha on both sides + short rebate (interest earned on short proceeds) − borrowing costs. Variants: fundamental (Tiger cubs, Viking, Lone Pine), sector specialists (tech, healthcare), activist, and equity market neutral (net ≈ 0, often quantitative). What makes them win: superior research, catalysts, good shorts (frauds, structural losers). What makes them lose: crowding (everyone owns the same longs and shorts), factor rotations (e.g. a sudden "junk rally" where heavily shorted bad companies jump), short squeezes.

2. Global macro

Bets on interest rates, currencies, equity indices and commodities based on macroeconomic and political views. Discretionary macro (Soros, Druckenmiller, Tudor, Brevan Howard, Caxton) uses judgement; systematic macro uses models. What drives returns: big turning points in policy and inflation, divergence between central banks, currency crises. Macro shines when rates and inflation move a lot (2022) and suffers in calm, policy-pinned markets (2010s zero rates).

3. Managed futures / CTAs (trend following)

Systematic strategies that buy assets in uptrends and sell those in downtrends across 50–100+ futures markets (Man AHL, Winton, Aspect, AQR). Their returns come from persistent trends (time-series momentum). Strongly positive in long crises (2008, 2022) — "crisis alpha" — but lose in choppy, trendless markets and at sharp reversals.

4. Relative value and arbitrage

  • Fixed income arbitrage: exploit small price differences between related bonds and derivatives. Example: the Treasury basis trade — buy a cash government bond, sell the futures contract, earn the small difference, using high leverage through repo. Profitable and steady… until funding dries up or margins rise (March 2020 dash for cash, LTCM 1998).
  • Convertible arbitrage: buy a company's convertible bond, short its shares to hedge — earns from cheap embedded options and volatility.
  • Volatility arbitrage: trade differences between implied and realised volatility, or across options.
  • Statistical arbitrage: quantitative trading of thousands of small mean-reverting price relationships.

Relative value strategies look like "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller": many small gains, occasional large losses when leverage is forced to unwind.

5. Event-driven

Profit from corporate events: mergers, spin-offs, restructurings, bankruptcies, activism.

Example: merger arbitrage

Company A offers CHF 50 per share in cash for company B. B trades at 48 (it was at 40 before the offer). The spread of 2 is a 4.2% return if the deal closes — about 8.5% annualised if it closes in six months. If the deal fails, B could fall back to 40: a −16.7% loss. The market-implied probability of completion is \((48 - 40)/(50 - 40) = 80\%\). The arbitrageur's edge is judging that probability better (regulatory, antitrust, financing risk) and diversifying across many deals.

  • Distressed debt (Elliott, Oaktree, Baupost): buy bonds of troubled companies cheaply, often taking control in restructuring.
  • Activism (Elliott, Pershing Square, Trian, Cevian in Europe): buy a stake and push management for changes (spin-offs, buybacks, new board members).

6. Credit long/short

Long and short positions in corporate bonds, loans and credit default swaps; capital structure arbitrage (long the bond, short the equity of the same company).

7. Quantitative

Computer models across horizons: high-frequency, statistical arbitrage, factor and machine-learning strategies (Renaissance, Two Sigma, D. E. Shaw, AQR, Man, Qube, Squarepoint). Edge comes from data, research infrastructure and execution; risks are crowding and regime change.

8. Multi-strategy and multi-manager "pod" platforms

Millennium, Citadel, Balyasny, Point72, ExodusPoint and others allocate capital to dozens or hundreds of independent teams ("pods"), each with tight risk limits — typically a pod's capital is cut after a drawdown of around 5% and the team is let go near 7–10%. Central risk management keeps the overall portfolio close to market-neutral, hedging out factor exposures, and uses leverage to turn many small alphas into a steady return. Pass-through fees and huge pay for top traders make them expensive, but their smooth returns have attracted a record $900 billion. Risks: the talent war, crowding in the same trades across platforms, dependence on leverage from prime brokers.

3.3 Leverage, crowding and how hedge funds blow up

  • Leverage through margin, repo, futures and swaps multiplies both gains and losses. A 2% loss on a 10× levered position wipes out 20% of capital.
  • Funding risk: prime brokers can raise margin requirements in a crisis, forcing sales at the worst moment.
  • Crowding and deleveraging spirals: when many funds hold the same positions, one fund's forced selling hurts the others, who then sell too (August 2007 quant quake, March 2020, the 2021 "momentum crash").
  • Liquidity mismatch: investors can redeem monthly while assets take months to sell — the reason for gates and lock-ups.
  • Hidden leverage: total return swaps let Archegos build huge positions across several banks without any one seeing the total.
  • Operational and fraud risk: Madoff (fake returns, no independent custodian), mis-marking of illiquid assets.

3.4 Judging performance honestly

  • Database biases: funds report voluntarily — survivorship bias (dead funds disappear) and backfill bias (funds add their good early history when they start reporting) make index returns look better than reality.
  • Beta in disguise: regress returns on market, credit, volatility and factor indices; what is left is alpha. Short-volatility strategies show smooth returns until a crash.
  • Smoothed returns in illiquid strategies understate volatility and correlation.
  • Dispersion: the gap between top and bottom managers is huge — manager selection matters more than the asset class.
  • Persistence: some evidence that top managers persist, especially in specialised strategies, but capacity limits returns as funds grow (Medallion is closed to outsiders for this reason).

3.5 How allocators select a hedge fund

  1. Investment due diligence: the edge (why will it persist?), process, team, track record decomposed into alpha and beta, risk management, capacity.
  2. Operational due diligence: independent administrator and custodian, auditor quality, valuation policy, compliance, cybersecurity, key-person risk.
  3. Terms: fees, liquidity, transparency (position-level reporting), alignment (does the manager invest own money?).
  4. Portfolio fit: correlation with existing holdings and the role (diversifier, return enhancer, crisis hedge).
Careers

Entry paths: investment banking or equity research → analyst at a long/short fund; trading or quant PhD → quant fund; economics and rates trading → macro. Multi-managers hire analysts who support a portfolio manager, then promote to PM with their own capital and strict drawdown limits. Pay depends heavily on performance.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. A fund has 120 long and 80 short on 100 of capital. What are its gross and net exposure?

Gross 200%, net 40%.

2. Target trades at 46, offer at 50, pre-deal price 38. Implied probability of completion?

(46 − 38)/(50 − 38) = 67%.

3. Why did LTCM and the March 2020 basis trade suffer despite "low-risk" trades?

Very high leverage on small spreads; when liquidity vanished and margins rose, forced selling widened spreads further — funding risk, not the trade logic, caused the losses.

4. What is a high-water mark?

The previous peak NAV; no performance fee is charged until the fund recovers above it.

5. Why do trend-following CTAs often do well in long crises?

Long crises produce persistent trends (falling stocks, rising bonds or commodities) that their systems follow; they suffer in sharp V-shaped reversals.

Part 5 · GlossaryKey terms

Absolute return
Aiming for positive returns regardless of market direction.
Activism
Buying a stake to push a company to change.
Basis trade
Buying a bond and selling its futures to capture a small price difference with leverage.
CTA
Commodity trading advisor; manager of systematic futures strategies.
Gate
Limit on how much investors can redeem at once.
Gross / net exposure
Total long + short / long − short as a share of capital.
High-water mark
Previous peak value above which performance fees apply.
Lock-up
Period during which investors cannot redeem.
Merger arbitrage
Trading the spread between a target's price and the offer price.
Multi-manager / pod
Platform allocating capital to many independent teams under central risk control.
Pass-through fees
Operating costs charged directly to investors.
Prime broker
Bank providing leverage, securities lending, custody and execution.
Short selling
Selling a borrowed security to buy it back later at a lower price.
