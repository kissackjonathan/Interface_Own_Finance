Part 1 · In briefWhat is a stock, and what does "analysing" it mean?

A stock (or share, or equity) is a small piece of ownership in a company. If a company has 100 million shares and you own 1,000, you own one hundred-thousandth of everything the company has and everything it will earn — after everyone else has been paid. That last part is important: employees, suppliers, the tax office and lenders are paid first. Shareholders get what is left. This is why we call equity a residual claim.

Because you own what is left, the value of a share is simply this:

The one idea to remember

A stock is worth the cash it will give to its owners in the future, brought back to today's value. Everything else — ratios, charts, news — is only a tool to estimate that future cash, how certain it is, and what the market already expects.

The price you see on the screen moves for two reasons only:

  1. The expected profits change (the company sells more, margins improve, a new product works…).
  2. The price the market is willing to pay for each franc of profit changes (interest rates move, fear or greed changes, the company looks more or less risky).

Professionals study a stock with three main "lenses":

  • Fundamental analysis — understand the business, read the financial statements, estimate future cash flows and the fair value. Question: "What is this company really worth?"
  • Quantitative analysis — use data and statistics on thousands of stocks to find the characteristics (called factors) that explain returns: cheapness, quality, momentum, risk. Question: "What do the numbers say, compared with all other stocks?"
  • Market analysis (sentiment, flows, technicals) — understand what other investors expect and how they are positioned. Question: "What is already in the price, and what could make it move?"

A good portfolio manager combines all three. You make money on a stock when you are right and the market is wrong, and the market later realises it. Being right about a great company is not enough if everybody already knows it is great — the price already reflects it. This is the most important lesson of this course, and we will come back to it again and again.

A simple picture

Imagine a lemonade stand that earns CHF 1,000 of cash per year, forever, with no growth. If you want a 10% return on your money, you would pay CHF 10,000 for it (because 1,000 / 10,000 = 10%). If the stand starts growing, you would pay more. If the neighbourhood becomes dangerous and the profits uncertain, you would demand more than 10% and so pay less. That is the whole logic of stock valuation, in one lemonade stand.

Part 2 · HistoryFrom the first share to quantitative investing

Stock analysis did not appear in one day. Each generation added a tool, often after a crisis showed that the old tools were not enough. Knowing this history helps you understand why professionals use the tools they use today.

Key dates

  1. 1602The Dutch East India Company (VOC) issues shares to the public in Amsterdam. It is usually called the first modern publicly traded company. Investors could buy and sell their shares to others — the birth of the stock market.
  2. 1792The Buttonwood Agreement: 24 brokers in New York agree on rules for trading securities. This becomes the New York Stock Exchange.
  3. 1896Charles Dow publishes the Dow Jones Industrial Average, one of the first stock indices. For the first time, people can follow "the market" with one number.
  4. 1920sDuPont analysis: Donaldson Brown, at the DuPont company, breaks the return on capital into margin × asset turnover. This is still how analysts explain profitability.
  5. 1929The Wall Street Crash. Prices had been driven by speculation and borrowed money. Many companies published little or misleading information.
  6. 1933–34Securities Acts and creation of the SEC in the United States. Companies must now publish audited financial statements. Without reliable data, fundamental analysis is impossible — this is its foundation.
  7. 1934Benjamin Graham and David Dodd publish Security Analysis, the founding book of fundamental analysis: study the numbers, estimate intrinsic value, buy with a "margin of safety".
  8. 1938John Burr Williams, The Theory of Investment Value: the value of a stock is the present value of its future dividends. This is the ancestor of every DCF model.
  9. 1949Graham publishes The Intelligent Investor, with the famous character "Mr. Market", a moody partner who offers you a different price every day.
  10. 1952Harry Markowitz publishes "Portfolio Selection": risk must be measured at the level of the whole portfolio. Diversification becomes a science.
  11. 1956–59Myron Gordon (with Eli Shapiro) formalises the growth model: \(P = D_1/(r-g)\). Still used every day.
  12. 1958Philip Fisher, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits: focus on the quality of the business and management, talk to clients, suppliers and competitors ("scuttlebutt").
  13. 1958–61Modigliani and Miller show that, under ideal conditions, the value of a firm does not depend on how it is financed or on its dividend policy. It forces analysts to focus on the cash the business generates.
  14. 1964William Sharpe (and Lintner, Treynor, Mossin) create the CAPM: the expected return of a stock depends on its beta, its sensitivity to the market.
  15. 1965Warren Buffett takes control of Berkshire Hathaway. With Charlie Munger he moves from Graham's "cheap stocks" to "wonderful companies at a fair price".
  16. 1968Michael Jensen defines alpha (performance not explained by the market). Edward Altman creates the Z-score to predict bankruptcy. Ball and Brown show that prices keep drifting after earnings surprises.
  17. 1970Eugene Fama formalises the Efficient Market Hypothesis: prices already reflect available information, so beating the market is very hard.
  18. 1977–90Peter Lynch runs the Magellan fund at Fidelity and earns about 29% per year by "buying what you know" and doing intense company research.
  19. 1981Rolf Banz finds the size effect: small companies earned more than the CAPM predicts. The hunt for "anomalies" begins.
  20. 1988Jim Simons' Renaissance Technologies launches the Medallion fund, the most successful quantitative fund in history.
  21. 1992–93Fama and French publish the three-factor model (market, size, value). Jegadeesh and Titman document momentum.
  22. 1996Richard Sloan shows that companies whose profits are not backed by cash (high "accruals") underperform — the birth of earnings-quality analysis.
  23. 2000Joseph Piotroski publishes the F-score, a simple 9-point test of financial health that improves value investing.
  24. 2000–02The dot-com crash, then the Enron and WorldCom frauds. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) strengthens audits and executive responsibility.
  25. 2008The Global Financial Crisis reminds everyone that leverage and liquidity can destroy "cheap" companies.
  26. 2013–15Robert Novy-Marx shows that gross profitability predicts returns; Fama and French add profitability and investment factors (five-factor model).
  27. TodayFundamental and quantitative approaches merge ("quantamental"). Alternative data (credit-card spending, satellite images, web traffic) and AI tools are used to estimate company results before they are published.

What history teaches a PM

Each school found something true. Graham: price matters. Fisher and Buffett: quality and time matter. Fama: the market is hard to beat, so you need a real edge. Quants: systematic characteristics (cheapness, quality, momentum) pay over time. Mauboussin: always ask what the price already expects. A complete analyst uses all these lessons together.

Part 3 · The coreHow professionals really analyse a stock

This is the heart of the course. We follow the order a portfolio manager follows in real life: understand what you own, understand the business, read the numbers, judge quality and risk, value the company, check what the quantitative data says, and finally build a thesis and size the position.

3.1 What you actually own: market cap and enterprise value

Market capitalisation is the value of all the shares: share price × number of shares. It is the value of the equity only.

But a company is financed by shareholders and lenders. If you bought the whole company, you would also take over its debt — and its cash. That is why professionals use enterprise value (EV), the value of the whole business, whoever finances it:

Formula · Enterprise value \[ \text{EV} = \text{Market cap} + \text{Debt} + \text{Preferred shares} + \text{Minority interests} - \text{Cash} \]

Debt includes bank loans, bonds and (under IFRS 16) lease liabilities. Minority interests are the parts of subsidiaries owned by others. We subtract cash because the buyer gets it back.

Rule of thumb: use EV with measures that belong to everyone (sales, EBITDA, EBIT, free cash flow to the firm). Use market cap with measures that belong only to shareholders (net income, dividends, equity). Mixing them is one of the most common beginner mistakes.

Watch the share count too. Use the diluted number of shares, which includes stock options, convertible bonds and restricted stock that will become shares. A company that gives a lot of stock to employees "dilutes" existing owners every year.

3.2 The equation of a stock price

Any stock price can be written as profit per share times a multiple:

\[ P = \text{EPS} \times \frac{P}{E} \]

EPS (earnings per share) is net profit divided by the number of shares. P/E (price-to-earnings ratio) is how many francs investors pay for one franc of annual profit. A P/E of 20 means you pay 20 years of today's profit.

From this, the return you get from a stock over a period comes from only three (or four) sources. This is often called the Bogle return decomposition, after Vanguard founder John Bogle:

Formula · Where stock returns come from \[ \text{Total return} \approx \underbrace{\frac{D}{P}}_{\text{dividend yield}} + \underbrace{g_{\text{EPS}}}_{\text{profit growth}} + \underbrace{\Delta \tfrac{P}{E}}_{\text{change in multiple}} \]

Buybacks enter through EPS growth: when a company buys back its own shares, the profit is shared among fewer shares, so EPS grows faster than total profit. Some analysts write "shareholder yield" = dividend yield + net buyback yield.

Example

A stock trades at a P/E of 15 with a 2% dividend yield. Over 5 years EPS grows 8% per year and the P/E rises to 18. The multiple change adds about \((18/15)^{1/5} - 1 \approx 3.7\%\) per year. Total ≈ 2% + 8% + 3.7% ≈ 13.7% per year. If instead the P/E falls to 12, the multiple removes about 4.4% per year and the total drops to about 5.6%, even though the company did exactly the same thing. Lesson: the price you pay matters as much as the business you buy.

3.3 Reading the three financial statements

Every listed company publishes three statements. In Switzerland large companies use IFRS (some use Swiss GAAP FER); US companies use US GAAP. The ideas are the same. Always read the annual report (the 10-K in the US) and the notes — the important details are often in the notes.

It shows what the company earned over a period:

The balance sheet

It is a photo at one date of what the company owns and owes:

\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Shareholders' equity} \]

Assets: cash, receivables (money clients owe), inventories, factories and equipment, intangibles and goodwill (the premium paid in past acquisitions). Liabilities: payables, debt, leases, pensions, provisions. Equity is what is left for shareholders — its book value.

The cash flow statement

It shows where cash really came from and went. It has three parts: operating cash flow (CFO, cash from the business), investing (capital expenditure "capex", acquisitions) and financing (debt, dividends, buybacks). Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. When profit and cash disagree for a long time, be careful.

Free cash flow: the number that matters most

Free cash flow (FCF) is the cash left after the company has paid everything needed to keep and grow the business. There are two versions:

Formulas · Free cash flow \[ \text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT}\,(1-t) + \text{D}\&\text{A} - \text{Capex} - \Delta\text{NWC} \] \[ \text{FCFE} = \text{FCFF} - \text{Interest}\,(1-t) + \text{Net borrowing} \]

FCFF (free cash flow to the firm) belongs to all investors → value it with WACC to get the EV. FCFE (to equity) belongs only to shareholders → value it with the cost of equity to get the market cap. \(t\) is the tax rate, D&A is depreciation and amortisation, \(\Delta\)NWC is the increase in net working capital (receivables + inventories − payables). EBIT × (1 − t) is called NOPAT: net operating profit after tax.

A quick version used by many analysts: FCF = Operating cash flow − Capex.

Quality of earnings: is the profit real?

Accounting gives managers choices. A professional checks that reported profit is backed by cash and is repeatable:

  • Cash conversion = FCF / Net income. Above ~80–90% over several years is healthy. Consistently low conversion is a red flag.
  • Accruals: the part of profit that is not cash. Sloan (1996) showed that firms with high accruals tend to underperform. A simple version: \[ \text{Accruals ratio} = \frac{\text{Net income} - \text{CFO}}{\text{Average total assets}} \]
  • Receivables growing faster than sales: the company may be selling on easy credit, or booking revenue too early.
  • Inventories growing faster than sales: demand may be weakening.
  • "Adjusted" profit far above official profit: check what is removed. Restructuring costs every year are not "one-off". Stock-based compensation is a real cost — it dilutes you.
  • Capitalised costs: if the company puts expenses on the balance sheet (capitalised software, development costs), profits look higher today.
  • Changes of auditor, CFO departures, late filings: classic warning signs.

Messod Beneish's M-score (1999) combines eight such ratios to estimate the probability that a company manipulates its earnings. It famously flagged Enron before its collapse.

3.4 Understanding the business and its moat

Numbers describe the past. To forecast the future you must understand how the company makes money and why competitors cannot simply copy it. Ask these questions:

  • What does it sell, to whom, and why do clients choose it? Split revenue by product, region and client type.
  • Unit economics: what does it cost to win a client, how much does one client bring over its lifetime, how fast do clients leave? (In software: CAC, LTV, churn, net revenue retention.)
  • Market size and share: how big is the total market (TAM), is it growing, is the company winning share?
  • Cost structure: how much of the cost is fixed? High fixed costs create operating leverage — profits rise faster than sales in good times and fall faster in bad times.
Formula · Degree of operating leverage \[ \text{DOL} = \frac{\%\Delta\,\text{EBIT}}{\%\Delta\,\text{Sales}} \]

A DOL of 3 means a 10% drop in sales causes a ~30% drop in operating profit.

Competitive advantage: the "moat"

Buffett's image: a business is a castle, and the moat protects its profits from attackers. Economic theory says that high profits attract competitors, who push returns down to the cost of capital. A company keeps high returns only if something stops this. The main sources of moat are:

MoatHow it worksExamples (type)
Network effectsThe product becomes more valuable as more people use it.Payment networks, marketplaces, exchanges
Switching costsIt is painful, risky or expensive for clients to change supplier.Enterprise software, banking systems, medical devices
Intangible assetsBrands, patents, licences, regulation.Luxury brands, pharma patents
Cost advantageProducing cheaper than anyone thanks to scale, location or process.Large retailers, low-cost producers
Efficient scaleThe market is only big enough for one or two players.Airports, pipelines, niche industrial suppliers

Michael Porter's five forces (1979) give a checklist for the industry: power of suppliers, power of buyers, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, and rivalry between competitors. An industry where all five are weak is a great place to be.

The proof of a moat is in the numbers: high and stable returns on capital over many years, stable or rising gross margins, and market share that holds.

Management and capital allocation

A CEO's most important job is to decide where each franc of free cash flow goes. There are only five options: reinvest in the business, acquire other companies, pay down debt, pay dividends, buy back shares. Good allocators invest when returns are high and return cash when they are not. Check: how are managers paid (on EPS growth? on ROIC? on share price?), do they own shares, did past acquisitions create value, do they buy back stock when it is cheap or when it is expensive?

3.5 Profitability and return on capital

Margins tell you how much profit is made per franc of sales. But a business with a 5% margin can be excellent (a supermarket that turns its assets very fast) and one with a 30% margin can be poor (if it needs huge investments). The real question is: how much profit does the company make for each franc invested in it?

ROE and the DuPont decomposition

\[ \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Equity}} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Assets}}}_{\text{asset turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{leverage}} \]

This tells you why ROE is high. A high ROE from margin and turnover is quality. A high ROE from leverage (lots of debt) is risk. The 5-step version also separates the tax burden (Net income/EBT) and the interest burden (EBT/EBIT) from the operating margin (EBIT/Sales).

ROIC: the king of ratios

Formula · Return on invested capital \[ \text{ROIC} = \frac{\text{NOPAT}}{\text{Invested capital}} = \frac{\text{EBIT}\,(1-t)}{\text{Net working capital} + \text{Net fixed assets} + \text{Goodwill}} \]

Invested capital can also be computed from the financing side: Equity + Net debt (+ leases). ROIC does not depend on how the firm is financed, which makes it the best measure of the business itself.

The rule that creates value

A company creates value only when ROIC > WACC (its cost of capital, see 3.7). If ROIC is 15% and WACC 8%, every franc invested creates value. If ROIC is 6% and WACC 8%, growth destroys value: the more the company grows, the poorer shareholders become. This is why growth alone is never enough.

This idea is measured by Economic Value Added (EVA, popularised by Stern Stewart in the 1990s):

\[ \text{EVA} = (\text{ROIC} - \text{WACC}) \times \text{Invested capital} \]

Growth comes from reinvestment

A company can only grow its profits by reinvesting. The link is simple and powerful:

\[ g = \text{Reinvestment rate} \times \text{ROIC} \qquad\text{where}\qquad \text{Reinvestment rate} = \frac{\text{Net investment}}{\text{NOPAT}} \]

With ROIC of 20%, reinvesting 30% of NOPAT gives 6% growth and leaves 70% as free cash flow. With ROIC of 10%, the same 6% growth needs 60% reinvestment and leaves only 40%. Same growth, very different value. For shareholders, the equivalent is the sustainable growth rate \(g = \text{ROE} \times b\), where \(b\) is the share of profit retained (not paid as dividends).

3.7 Valuation: what is the stock worth?

There are two families of valuation. Relative valuation compares the price with similar companies ("is it cheap compared with its peers?"). Intrinsic valuation estimates the value from the company's own future cash flows ("what is it worth on its own?"). Professionals use both and investigate when they disagree. (A full dedicated Valuation course goes even further: sum-of-the-parts, LBO, options.)

The cost of capital: the return investors demand

To bring future cash back to today, we need a discount rate: the return investors require for the risk they take. For shareholders it is the cost of equity, usually estimated with the CAPM:

Formula · CAPM cost of equity \[ r_E = r_f + \beta \times \text{ERP} \]

\(r_f\) = risk-free rate (yield of a long government bond in the currency of the cash flows: about 0.5–1% for the Swiss Confederation, roughly 4% for US Treasuries in recent years — always check today's value). \(\text{ERP}\) = equity risk premium, the extra return investors demand for owning stocks instead of bonds, historically about 4–6%. \(\beta\) = the stock's sensitivity to the market:

\[ \beta = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)} \]

β = 1: moves like the market. β = 1.5: tends to move 50% more. β = 0.6: defensive.

Beta measured from past prices is noisy. Professionals often use the average beta of the industry, removing debt ("unlevered beta") and adding back the target company's own debt (Hamada formula):

\[ \beta_L = \beta_U \left[ 1 + (1-t)\frac{D}{E} \right] \]

The company's total cost of capital mixes equity and debt:

Formula · WACC (weighted average cost of capital) \[ \text{WACC} = \frac{E}{D+E}\, r_E + \frac{D}{D+E}\, r_D\,(1-t) \]

\(E\), \(D\) = market values of equity and debt · \(r_D\) = cost of debt (yield on the company's bonds) · \((1-t)\) because interest is tax-deductible. Typical WACC for a large stable company: 6–9%.

Discounting: why a franc tomorrow is worth less than a franc today

\[ PV = \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} \]

CHF 100 received in 5 years at a 8% discount rate is worth \(100/1.08^5 = 68.1\) today. The further away and the riskier the cash, the less it is worth now.

The DCF model, step by step

A discounted cash flow (DCF) model adds up the present value of all future free cash flows:

Formula · DCF \[ \text{EV} = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{\text{FCFF}_t}{(1+\text{WACC})^t} + \frac{TV_N}{(1+\text{WACC})^N} \qquad TV_N = \frac{\text{FCFF}_{N+1}}{\text{WACC} - g} \]

\(TV\) is the terminal value: the value of all cash flows after year \(N\), assuming they grow forever at a stable rate \(g\) (Gordon growth). \(g\) must be below long-term nominal GDP growth (usually 1.5–3%) — no company can grow faster than the economy forever.

  1. Forecast revenue for 5–10 years: market growth, market share, price.
  2. Forecast margins: where will EBIT margin go as the company grows? Compare with the best competitors.
  3. Forecast reinvestment: capex and working capital needed. Check that implied ROIC is realistic.
  4. Compute FCFF each year and discount with WACC.
  5. Terminal value with a stable growth rate (or an exit multiple, as a cross-check).
  6. From EV to equity value: EV − net debt − minorities − other claims (pensions, leases).
  7. Divide by diluted shares → value per share. Compare with the price.
  8. Sensitivity analysis: change WACC, growth and margins. A DCF gives a range, not a single truth.

The value driver formula

McKinsey's valuation team summarises the link between growth, ROIC and value in one line:

\[ \text{Value} = \frac{\text{NOPAT}_{t+1}\left(1 - \dfrac{g}{\text{RONIC}}\right)}{\text{WACC} - g} \]

RONIC = return on new invested capital. If RONIC = WACC, growth adds nothing: the value is the same as with zero growth. Try it: growth only creates value when new investments earn more than the cost of capital.

Other intrinsic models

  • Dividend discount model (Gordon): \(P_0 = \dfrac{D_1}{r_E - g}\). Good for stable dividend payers (utilities, mature consumer staples).
  • Residual income model: value = book value + present value of profits above the cost of equity. Very useful for banks and insurers, where cash flow is hard to define: \[ V_0 = B_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{(\text{ROE}_t - r_E)\, B_{t-1}}{(1+r_E)^t} \]
  • Reverse DCF (expectations investing): instead of guessing the value, start from today's price and solve for the growth and margins the market implies. Then ask: is that realistic? This is often the most useful DCF of all.

Multiples are not random: they come from the same fundamentals as the DCF. For a stable company:

Formulas · Justified multiples \[ \frac{P_0}{E_1} = \frac{\text{Payout ratio}}{r_E - g} \qquad\qquad \frac{P_0}{B_0} = \frac{\text{ROE} - g}{r_E - g} \]

A company deserves a higher P/E when it grows faster, is less risky (lower \(r_E\)) and needs less reinvestment (higher payout for the same growth, i.e. higher ROIC). A company deserves a P/B above 1 only if ROE > cost of equity.

Classic traps
  • Value trap: a low P/E because profits are about to fall (cyclical peak, disruption, debt). Cyclicals often look cheapest at the top of the cycle, when profits are highest.
  • Bad peer group: comparing a 25% ROIC company with a 7% ROIC company and calling the first "expensive".
  • Mixing EV and equity measures: e.g. EV / net income. Always match numerator and denominator.
  • Trailing vs forward: compare the same period for all companies (e.g. next 12 months consensus).
  • Terminal growth ≥ WACC: the formula explodes. Always keep \(g\) well below WACC.

3.8 Quantitative analysis: what the data says

Quantitative analysis looks at thousands of stocks at the same time and asks which measurable characteristics have historically been linked to higher returns or lower risk. These characteristics are called factors. Even a purely fundamental investor must know them, because they explain a large part of every portfolio's performance.

The main equity factors

FactorHow it is measuredWhy it might work
Market (beta)Sensitivity to the indexReward for bearing stock market risk.
Value (HML)Book/price, earnings yield, FCF yield, EV/EBITRisk (cheap firms are often distressed) and behaviour (investors over-extrapolate bad news).
Size (SMB)Market cap (small minus big)Less liquid, less followed. Weaker since its discovery.
Momentum (UMD)Return over the last 12 months excluding the last month ("12-1")Investors under-react to news, then herd. Strong but crashes suddenly in sharp market rebounds.
Quality / profitability (RMW)ROIC, gross profit/assets, stable earnings, low debt, low accrualsInvestors underestimate how long great businesses stay great.
Investment (CMA)Asset growth (conservative minus aggressive)Firms that grow assets fast (often by acquisitions) tend to disappoint.
Low volatilityLow past volatility or betaMany investors cannot use leverage, so they overpay for high-beta "lottery" stocks.

Measuring alpha with a regression

To know whether a stock or a fund really outperformed, we remove what is explained by its factor exposures. The CAPM regression:

\[ R_{i,t} - r_{f,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i\,(R_{m,t} - r_{f,t}) + \varepsilon_{i,t} \]

And the Fama-French five-factor version (add Carhart's momentum UMD for six):

\[ R_i - r_f = \alpha + \beta_{M}\,\text{MKT} + s\,\text{SMB} + h\,\text{HML} + r\,\text{RMW} + c\,\text{CMA} + \varepsilon \]

α (alpha) is the return not explained by the factors — the true skill, or luck. The coefficients (\(\beta, s, h, r, c\)) are the exposures: they tell you if a "brilliant" manager was simply holding small, cheap or high-momentum stocks. Check the t-statistic of alpha: \(t = \hat\alpha / \text{SE}(\hat\alpha)\). Because researchers have tested hundreds of factors, Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue a new factor should have \(t > 3\), not just 2.

Risk and performance measures

Formulas · Risk-adjusted performance \[ \text{Sharpe} = \frac{R_p - r_f}{\sigma_p} \qquad \text{Sortino} = \frac{R_p - r_f}{\sigma_{\text{down}}} \qquad \text{IR} = \frac{R_p - R_b}{\text{TE}} \]

\(\sigma_p\) = volatility (standard deviation of returns; annualise daily volatility by × \(\sqrt{252}\)) · \(\sigma_{\text{down}}\) = volatility of negative returns only · \(R_b\) = benchmark return · TE = tracking error = volatility of \(R_p - R_b\). Maximum drawdown = largest fall from a peak to a trough. A Sharpe of 0.5 is typical for the stock market over the long run; above 1 sustained is excellent.

The fundamental law of active management

Richard Grinold (1989) showed what drives a manager's information ratio:

\[ \text{IR} \approx \text{IC} \times \sqrt{\text{Breadth}} \]

IC (information coefficient) = correlation between your forecasts and what really happens — your skill per bet. Breadth = number of independent bets per year. A quant with a tiny edge (IC = 0.03) on 2,000 independent bets can beat a stock-picker with a big edge (IC = 0.15) on 20 bets. This is why fundamental PMs must have strong convictions on few names, and quants many small ones.

Building a quantitative score

  1. Choose metrics for each factor (e.g. value = average of EV/EBIT, FCF yield, P/B).
  2. Within each sector, convert each metric into a z-score: \(z = (x - \mu)/\sigma\), and limit extremes (winsorise at ±3).
  3. Average the z-scores into factor scores, then combine factors (e.g. value + quality + momentum).
  4. Rank stocks; the top group is your "long" list, the bottom group your "avoid/short" list.
  5. Backtest honestly, then monitor.
Backtesting traps
  • Look-ahead bias: using data that was not available on the date (annual reports come out weeks after year-end).
  • Survivorship bias: testing only on companies alive today forgets the ones that went bankrupt.
  • Data snooping: try 1,000 rules and some will look brilliant by pure luck.
  • Costs and liquidity: trading costs, market impact and short-selling fees can erase a paper profit.
  • Regime change: a factor that worked for 50 years can stop working once everyone uses it.

Earnings surprises and estimate revisions

Analysts publish forecasts; the average is the consensus. Prices react to the difference between results and consensus, not to the results themselves. A standard measure is the standardised unexpected earnings:

\[ \text{SUE} = \frac{\text{EPS}_{\text{actual}} - \text{EPS}_{\text{expected}}}{\sigma(\text{surprises})} \]

After a positive surprise, prices tend to keep drifting up for weeks (post-earnings announcement drift, Ball & Brown 1968, Bernard & Thomas 1989). Stocks whose consensus forecasts are being raised (positive revisions) tend to outperform. Watching revisions is one of the most practical tools for a PM.

3.9 Reading the market: expectations, sentiment and flows

A great analysis can still lose money if you ignore what is already in the price. Tools to understand the market's view:

  • Consensus and the "buy-side whisper": what do analysts and investors really expect for the next quarter and the next 3 years?
  • Valuation vs history and peers: a stock at its highest P/E in 10 years already prices in good news.
  • Positioning: share of hedge funds and long-only funds owning it; short interest (shares sold short / float) and days to cover. Crowded stocks fall hard when news disappoints.
  • Options market: implied volatility shows the expected move around results; skew shows fear.
  • Insider transactions: managers buying with their own money is a meaningful signal; selling much less so (they sell for many reasons).
  • Price trend: even fundamental investors look at the chart to avoid fighting a strong trend and to time entries. A stock that cannot rise on good news is telling you something.
Variant perception

The term comes from hedge fund manager Michael Steinhardt. Before buying, write in one sentence: "The market believes X. I believe Y, because Z. We will know who is right when W happens." If you cannot fill this sentence, you do not have an edge — you only have an opinion that is already in the price.

3.10 Building the investment thesis

Professional funds write a short investment memo before buying. It forces clear thinking and lets you check later whether you were right for the right reasons. A good structure:

  1. Thesis in 3 sentences: what the business is, why it is mispriced, what the upside is.
  2. Variant perception: what you see that the market does not.
  3. Valuation: base, bull and bear cases with probabilities, and the expected value. Example: bull 40 (25%), base 30 (50%), bear 18 (25%) → expected value = 0.25×40 + 0.5×30 + 0.25×18 = 29.5.
  4. Catalysts: events that will make the market change its mind (results, new product, restructuring, spin-off, buyback, rate cut…) and when.
  5. Key risks and what would prove you wrong.
  6. Kill criteria: conditions that make you sell, decided before you buy.
  7. Position size and liquidity.

Position sizing

How much to buy is as important as what to buy. Common approaches:

  • Conviction × risk: size proportional to expected return divided by the stock's volatility, so each position contributes a similar amount of risk.
  • Kelly criterion (John Kelly, 1956): for a bet with win probability \(p\), loss probability \(q = 1-p\) and payoff \(b\) per unit risked, the growth-optimal fraction is \[ f^* = \frac{b\,p - q}{b} \] Full Kelly is very aggressive; professionals use a fraction (often a quarter or half) because estimates of \(p\) and \(b\) are uncertain.
  • Limits: maximum weight per stock (e.g. 5–8%), per sector, and maximum % of daily trading volume you can sell quickly.
Pre-mortem

Before buying, imagine it is two years later and the investment lost 40%. Write the most likely story of why. This simple exercise, proposed by psychologist Gary Klein, reveals risks your enthusiasm was hiding.

3.11 A portfolio manager's checklist

  1. Business: Can I explain how it makes money in two sentences? Who are the clients and why do they buy?
  2. Industry: Five forces — is it a good industry? Is it growing?
  3. Moat: What protects profits? Is ROIC high and stable over 10 years?
  4. Management: Track record of capital allocation? Incentives aligned? Do they own shares?
  5. Financials: Revenue growth, margins trend, FCF conversion, accruals, one-offs.
  6. Balance sheet: Net debt/EBITDA, maturities, Z-score, off-balance-sheet items.
  7. Valuation: DCF range, reverse DCF, multiples vs peers and history.
  8. Quant view: Value, quality, momentum, revision scores. Am I fighting the data? Why?
  9. Expectations: Consensus, positioning, what is priced in. My variant perception.
  10. Catalysts & timing: What will close the gap between price and value, and when?
  11. Risks: Pre-mortem, kill criteria, ESG and regulatory risks, currency exposure.
  12. Portfolio fit: Correlation with existing holdings, factor exposures, size.

3.12 Behavioural traps

The biggest enemy of the analyst is often their own brain. The most dangerous biases in stock analysis:

  • Confirmation bias: you look for information that supports your thesis. Fix: actively seek the bear case; talk to someone who is short the stock.
  • Anchoring: you stay fixed on the price you paid or a past high. The market does not know your purchase price.
  • Overconfidence: your forecast ranges are too narrow. Fix: scenarios and probabilities.
  • Narrative fallacy: a beautiful story replaces numbers. Fix: every story must show up in ROIC and FCF.
  • Loss aversion and the disposition effect: selling winners too early and keeping losers too long.
  • Extrapolation: assuming recent growth continues forever. Most high growth rates fade towards the average (mean reversion).
  • Endowment and sunk-cost effects: overvaluing what you own and the work you have done.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. A company has ROIC of 7% and WACC of 9%. Management announces a plan to double growth. Good news for shareholders?

No. Since ROIC < WACC, each franc invested earns less than its cost. Faster growth destroys more value. The company should first improve returns or return cash to shareholders.

2. Why should you use EV and not market cap with EBITDA?

EBITDA is earned before interest, so it belongs to both lenders and shareholders. EV is the value of the claims of both. Market cap is only the shareholders' part, so the ratio would be inconsistent and would make indebted companies look cheap.

3. Net income is 100, operating cash flow 60, for three years in a row. What do you think?

Earnings quality is weak: large accruals. Check receivables, inventories, capitalised costs and revenue recognition in the notes. This pattern often precedes earnings disappointments.

4. Using the justified P/E formula, what P/E does a company deserve with a 50% payout, cost of equity 8% and growth 4%?

\(P/E_1 = 0.5 / (0.08 - 0.04) = 12.5\).

5. A fund made 14% per year while the market made 10%. Its regression shows β = 1.3, a big positive HML (value) exposure and an alpha of 0.2% with t = 0.3. Is the manager skilled?

Probably not. The outperformance is explained by higher market risk (β 1.3) and a value tilt, which can be bought cheaply in a factor ETF. The alpha is tiny and not statistically significant.

6. A cyclical stock trades at a P/E of 6 at the top of the economic cycle. Is it cheap?

Maybe not. Its profits are probably at a peak. Use normalised (average-cycle) earnings, or EV/Sales with average margins. Cyclicals often look cheapest when they are most dangerous.

7. What two things move a stock's price?

Changes in expected future earnings/cash flows, and changes in the multiple (the price paid per franc of profit, driven by rates, risk and sentiment).

8. Fill the variant perception sentence for a stock you know.

"The market believes … . I believe … because … . We will know who is right when … ." If you cannot complete it, you have not yet found an edge.

Operating profit before interest and tax / also before depreciation and amortisation.

Value of the whole business: market cap + net debt + other claims.

Extra return demanded for holding stocks instead of risk-free bonds.

A characteristic shared by many stocks that explains returns (value, momentum, quality…).

Cash left after operating costs, taxes and investments.

Active return divided by tracking error.
Moat
A durable competitive advantage protecting profits.
NOPAT
Net operating profit after tax = EBIT × (1 − tax rate).

Profits move more than sales because of fixed costs.

Return on equity / return on all invested capital.

Excess return per unit of volatility.

Value of all cash flows after the explicit forecast period.
Value trap
A stock that looks cheap but deserves to be, because its profits will fall.

A well-founded view that differs from the market's consensus.

Weighted average cost of capital of equity and debt.

Receivables + inventories − payables: cash tied up in daily operations.
