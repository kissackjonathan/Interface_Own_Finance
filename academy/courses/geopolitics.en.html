Part 1 · In briefWhy a portfolio manager must think about geopolitics

Geopolitics is the study of how power, geography and politics between states interact: wars, sanctions, trade conflicts, alliances, revolutions, elections, control of resources and of sea routes. For an investor, geopolitics matters because it can suddenly change cash flows (a pipeline is cut, a factory is sanctioned, a tariff hits margins) and, above all, the price of risk (investors demand a higher return to hold assets exposed to danger).

2026 is a textbook year. In January, the United States captured Venezuela's president Nicolás Maduro; on 28 February, US and Israeli strikes on Iran began a war that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei; from early March, Iran made the Strait of Hormuz — through which about 20 million barrels of oil a day passed in 2025 — almost impassable. Brent crude went from about $71 the day before the war to more than $120 at times in the spring, the IEA organised the largest emergency oil release in its history (400 million barrels), the UAE left OPEC — yet the S&P 500, after a fall of about 9%, reached new records within months.

The one idea to remember

Markets price risk (dangers whose probabilities can be estimated) well, but struggle with uncertainty (dangers whose probabilities nobody really knows). Geopolitical shocks are mostly uncertainty. Typically, prices overreact at first (fear, forced selling, liquidity crunch), then recover once investors can put numbers on the scenarios. The shocks that leave lasting damage are those that change the economy itself: energy supply, inflation, interest rates and recession.

A simple picture

A coin toss is risk: you do not know the result, but you know the odds (50/50). Being asked to bet on whether a new Iranian leader will reopen a strait is uncertainty: you do not even know the odds. Faced with a coin, people bet calmly. Faced with the unknown, they demand a big discount to take part — or refuse to play. That discount is the geopolitical risk premium.

Part 2 · HistoryA century of shocks and market reactions

  1. 1914Outbreak of the First World War: the New York Stock Exchange closes for about four months to prevent panic selling by European investors.
  2. 1921Frank Knight publishes Risk, Uncertainty and Profit, separating measurable risk from unmeasurable uncertainty.
  3. 1962Cuban missile crisis: the world comes close to nuclear war; US stocks fall during the tense days, then recover quickly once the crisis ends.
  4. 1973–74Yom Kippur War and Arab oil embargo: the oil price roughly quadruples. Combined with inflation, it triggers one of the worst bear markets of the century (S&P 500 about −48%).
  5. 1979–80Iranian revolution and Iran–Iraq war: second oil shock, inflation, then Volcker's very high interest rates.
  6. 1990Iraq invades Kuwait: the oil price more than doubles in a few months, US stocks fall about 20%, the US enters recession. Stocks rally as soon as the air war begins in January 1991.
  7. 11 Sep 2001Terrorist attacks: US markets close for four days; the S&P 500 loses about 12% in the first week, then recovers its level within about two months.
  8. 2003Iraq war: markets fall during the build-up, then rise strongly when the invasion begins — the origin of the saying "sell the build-up, buy the invasion".
  9. 2014Russia annexes Crimea: first Western sanctions; the rouble and Russian assets fall, global markets barely react.
  10. 2016Brexit referendum: the pound suffers one of its largest daily falls ever; global equities recover within weeks.
  11. 2018–19US–China trade war: successive tariff rounds; markets react to every tweet.
  12. 2022Russia invades Ukraine: Brent briefly near $139, European gas at record levels in summer, the West freezes about half of Russia's foreign reserves and cuts Russian banks from SWIFT. The 2022 bear market comes mostly from the inflation and rate shock that followed.
  13. 2025"Liberation Day" (2 April): sweeping US tariffs; stocks fall more than 10% in a week, more than in many wars.
  14. 2026Capture of Maduro (3 January); US withdraws from 66 international organisations (7 January); US–Israel war on Iran from 28 February; Hormuz closed from 4 March; Qatar's LNG plants hit; record IEA stock release; the UAE leaves OPEC (1 May); peace talks on Ukraine and Iran in November.

3.2 The six channels from geopolitics to prices

  1. The discount rate (risk premium): investors demand a higher equity risk premium (ERP) when the future is more uncertain. Even if earnings do not change, prices fall.
  2. Cash flows: supply disruption (oil, gas, chips, grain), sanctions, tariffs, expropriation, destroyed assets, lost markets.
  3. Inflation and central banks: an energy shock raises inflation; if central banks raise rates (or stop cutting), all assets are hit. This is the channel that turned 1973 and 2022 into bear markets.
  4. Currencies and safe havens: money flows to the dollar, Swiss franc, gold and government bonds; emerging-market currencies fall.
  5. Liquidity and positioning: leveraged funds cut risk at the same time, amplifying the first move (VIX spikes, correlations jump towards 1).
  6. Policy response: emergency oil releases, rate cuts, fiscal support, ceasefires. Often the turning point of markets.
Formula · Why a higher risk premium lowers prices \[ P = \frac{D}{r - g} \qquad r = r_f + ERP \]

\(P\) price, \(D\) next dividend, \(r\) required return (risk-free rate \(r_f\) + equity risk premium), \(g\) long-term growth. Example: \(D = 4\), \(r = 8\%\), \(g = 4\%\) → \(P = 4/0.04 = 100\). If war fears raise the ERP by 0.5 point: \(P = 4/0.045 = 88.9\), a fall of 11% without any change in dividends. If fears fade, the premium falls and prices recover — the classic "V".

Pricing scenarios: the market as a probability machine

Formula · Scenario-weighted price \[ E[P] = p \cdot P_{\text{bad}} + (1-p) \cdot P_{\text{good}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad p = \frac{P_{\text{market}} - P_{\text{good}}}{P_{\text{bad}} - P_{\text{good}}} \]

Example: an analyst thinks Brent goes to $85 if a peace deal reopens Hormuz, and to $115 if strikes resume. If Brent trades at $97, the market-implied probability of the bad scenario is \((97-85)/(115-85) = 40\%\). If you believe the true probability is 20%, oil is too expensive and the risk premium should shrink. This simple method turns headlines into numbers you can disagree with.

3.3 What history tells us about market reactions

  • Across dozens of geopolitical events since 1945, the average one-day fall of the S&P 500 is about 1%, and the typical total drawdown about 5–6%; the market usually bottoms within a few weeks and recovers within a few months.
  • The size of the event does not predict the size of the market reaction: 9/11 was horrific but its market effect was short; the 2025 tariffs had no shots fired but caused a larger fall.
  • The big exceptions are shocks that hit energy supply or trigger inflation and recession: 1973, 1979, 1990. That is why oil is the key transmission belt.
  • 2026 confirms the rule: the Iran war produced a historic oil shock, but the S&P 500 fell about 9% peak to trough (end of January to end of March) and recovered to records — partly because US tech earnings depend little on oil, and the US now produces a record ~13.8 million barrels a day.
  • Local markets suffer much more than global ones: Russian stocks in 2014 and 2022, Gulf shipping and airlines in 2026, Taiwan in any China crisis.

Survivorship warning

"Markets always recover" is true for the US index, which belongs to the winning side of history. Investors in Russia (1917, and again in 2022 for foreigners), China (1949) or Germany (1945) lost almost everything. Geopolitics matters most for where your assets are and who controls them — custody, legal jurisdiction, sanctions risk.

3.5 The main families of geopolitical risk

  • Wars and military conflicts: direct destruction, supply disruption, refugee flows, fiscal costs.
  • Chokepoints: Hormuz (oil and LNG of the Gulf), Malacca (Asia's energy imports), Bab el-Mandeb and Suez (Europe–Asia trade; Houthi attacks since late 2023), Bosphorus (Black Sea grain and oil), Panama (US trade), Taiwan Strait (chips and East Asian shipping).
  • Sanctions: asset freezes, bans on transactions, exclusion from SWIFT, oil price caps, secondary sanctions (punishing third countries' firms). They can make assets worthless for foreign holders overnight.
  • Trade wars and tariffs: hit margins, supply chains, and the dollar; favour "friend-shoring" and near-shoring.
  • Export controls and tech war: chips, AI, rare earths, gallium (see the Rare Earths course).
  • Regime change and expropriation: nationalisations (Iran 1951, Venezuela 2007), capture of Maduro 2026 reshaping Venezuelan oil.
  • Elections and political instability: policy uncertainty indices rise before contested elections.
  • Terrorism, cyberattacks, sabotage: Nord Stream pipelines (2022), undersea cables, ransomware on infrastructure.
  • Weaponised interdependence: using control of networks (dollar, SWIFT, chips, rare earths, LNG) as a weapon.

3.6 How professionals measure geopolitical risk

  • GPR index (Caldara–Iacoviello): counts newspaper articles about wars, threats and terrorism; spikes in 1990, 2001, 2003, 2022 and 2026.
  • Economic Policy Uncertainty index (Baker, Bloom, Davis), including trade-policy uncertainty.
  • Market prices: VIX (equity volatility), OVX (oil volatility), option skew, sovereign CDS spreads, oil futures curve (a steep backwardation signals current scarcity), war-risk insurance premiums, freight rates.
  • Prediction markets and polls: probabilities of deals, elections, ceasefires.
  • Specialised research: Eurasia Group's annual "Top Risks", bank geopolitical dashboards, think tanks (CSIS, IISS, Chatham House, Brookings, Crisis Group).
  • Physical data: tanker tracking (Kpler, Vortexa), satellite images, customs data.

3.7 The big structural themes in 2026

  1. US–China rivalry: chips, AI, rare earths, tariffs, Taiwan; a one-year truce after the Busan meeting (October 2025), but the tech decoupling continues.
  2. The Gulf after the Iran war: the region's risk profile has permanently changed — the world now knows Hormuz can be closed. Expect bypass pipelines, larger strategic stocks, higher insurance and a structural risk premium.
  3. Russia–Europe: war in Ukraine in its fifth year, US-brokered talks; Europe rearms and has cut Russian gas.
  4. America First and the retreat from multilateralism: tariffs, exit from WHO, UNESCO and dozens of bodies; alliances become transactional (see the International Organisations course).
  5. Multipolarity: expanded BRICS, middle powers (Gulf states, India, Turkey, Brazil) playing all sides; the UAE leaving OPEC shows even old blocs fracture.
  6. Resource nationalism: critical minerals, energy, food; governments take stakes and set price floors.
  7. Debt and fiscal dominance: higher defence and energy spending raises deficits — supports gold, weighs on long bonds.

3.8 The PM's toolkit for geopolitical risk

A disciplined process
  1. Map exposures: revenues, supply chains, assets and custody by country; energy intensity; sanctions exposure.
  2. Write scenarios (base, bad, very bad) with probabilities and market levels for oil, rates, FX, equities. Update the probabilities as news arrives.
  3. Find the transmission channel: is this a sentiment shock (fade it) or a supply/inflation shock (respect it)?
  4. Use base rates: most shocks are bought back within months; do not sell everything at the bottom.
  5. Hedge cheaply in advance, not in panic: gold, CHF, long-dated oil calls, index puts, energy equities as a natural hedge. When volatility is already high, hedges are expensive.
  6. Size positions for the scenario you cannot model; keep liquidity.
  7. Do not trade headlines without an edge: algorithms react in milliseconds.
The Swiss angle

Switzerland is neutral but not isolated: it adopted EU sanctions against Russia in 2022, it imports nearly all its oil and gas, and the franc tends to rise in crises — good for Swiss investors' safety, bad for exporters and for the value of foreign assets in CHF. The SNB watches the franc closely in every geopolitical shock.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. What is the difference between risk and uncertainty?

Risk: unknown outcome with known or estimable probabilities. Uncertainty: the probabilities themselves are unknown. Markets demand a larger premium for uncertainty.

2. Dividend 3, required return 7%, growth 3%. Price? And if the risk premium rises by 1 point?

3/0.04 = 75; 3/0.05 = 60: −20%.

3. Brent at $100; good scenario $80, bad scenario $120. Implied probability of the bad scenario?

(100 − 80)/(120 − 80) = 50%.

4. Why did 1973 cause a bear market while 9/11 did not?

1973 hit energy supply and caused inflation and recession; 9/11 was a fear shock without lasting damage to the economy's supply.

5. Why did the yen weaken during the 2026 Iran war despite its safe-haven reputation?

Japan imports nearly all its energy: higher oil worsens its trade balance, and the Fed kept rates high, supporting the dollar.
