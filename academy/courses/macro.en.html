Part 1 · In briefThe economy as a whole

Macroeconomics studies the economy as one big system: total production (GDP), growth, inflation, employment, interest rates, government budgets, trade and exchange rates. Microeconomics looks at the trees (one company, one market); macroeconomics looks at the forest.

Why should an investor care? Because macro sets the tide on which every asset floats. When growth and inflation change, so do interest rates, company profits, currencies and investors' appetite for risk. A brilliant stock-picker can still lose money in a recession or an inflation shock; a macro-aware investor knows which risks the portfolio carries.

The one idea to remember

Markets are driven by two macro forces above all: growth (is the economy speeding up or slowing down?) and inflation (are prices rising faster or slower?). Their combination defines four regimes, and each regime favours different assets. What matters is the direction of change and the surprise versus expectations, not the level.

A simple picture

Think of the economy as a car. GDP is the speed. Inflation is the engine temperature. The central bank controls the brake and the accelerator (interest rates); the government can add fuel (spending) or take it away (taxes). Drive too fast and the engine overheats (inflation); brake too hard and the car stalls (recession). Investors are passengers trying to guess the driver's next move.

Part 2 · HistoryBig ideas born from big crises

  1. 1776Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations: division of labour, markets and the "invisible hand".
  2. 1817David Ricardo: comparative advantage — countries gain from trade even when one is better at everything.
  3. 1911Irving Fisher formalises the quantity theory of money (\(MV = PY\)).
  4. 1929–33The Great Depression: US output falls by about a quarter, unemployment reaches about 25%. Old theories cannot explain it.
  5. 1933Irving Fisher, "debt-deflation": falling prices increase the real burden of debt, causing more defaults and more deflation.
  6. 1936John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory: demand can stay too low for long periods; governments should spend in recessions. Macroeconomics is born as a field.
  7. 1944Bretton Woods: fixed exchange rates linked to the dollar, creation of the IMF and the World Bank.
  8. 1956–58Robert Solow's growth model (long-run growth comes from technology); Bill Phillips finds a trade-off between unemployment and wage inflation (the Phillips curve).
  9. 1963–68Milton Friedman (with Anna Schwartz) blames the Depression on the Fed's monetary mistakes and argues that the Phillips trade-off disappears once people expect inflation.
  10. 1970sStagflation: oil shocks (1973, 1979), high inflation and high unemployment. Keynesian fine-tuning is discredited. Robert Lucas (1976) shows that policy changes alter people's behaviour ("Lucas critique").
  11. 1980sVolcker's disinflation; supply-side policies (Reagan, Thatcher); globalisation accelerates.
  12. 1985–2007The "Great Moderation": low inflation and mild recessions, credited to independent central banks and inflation targeting.
  13. 2008The Global Financial Crisis: a "Minsky moment" — after years of stability, debt-fuelled excess collapses. Central banks cut to zero and launch QE.
  14. 2013Larry Summers revives "secular stagnation": weak demand and low neutral rates for a long time.
  15. 2020The Covid shock: the fastest recession in history, then massive fiscal and monetary support.
  16. 2021–23Inflation returns (US above 9% in 2022, eurozone above 10%), forcing the fastest rate hikes in decades.
  17. 2025–26Large US tariffs (from April 2025) and the debate over deglobalisation, industrial policy and AI-driven investment shape the macro picture.

Part 3 · The coreHow the economy works — and how to use it as an investor

3.1 GDP: measuring the economy

Formula · GDP by spending \[ Y = C + I + G + (X - M) \]

\(C\) = household consumption (about 50–70% of GDP in rich countries), \(I\) = investment (machines, buildings, software, housing, inventories), \(G\) = government purchases, \(X - M\) = exports minus imports (net exports).

  • Nominal vs real GDP: real GDP removes inflation. Real growth = nominal growth − inflation (approximately).
  • GDP per capita measures living standards; Switzerland is among the highest in the world.
  • Potential GDP: what the economy can produce at full use of labour and capital without accelerating inflation. The output gap = (actual − potential)/potential. Positive gap → inflation pressure; negative → slack.
  • Switzerland: a small open economy; exports (pharma, chemicals, machinery, watches, financial services) are a large share of GDP, so the franc and world demand matter a lot.

3.2 Long-run growth

Formula · Production function and growth accounting \[ Y = A\,K^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad g_Y \approx g_A + \alpha\,g_K + (1-\alpha)\,g_L \]

Output depends on capital \(K\), labour \(L\) and productivity \(A\) (technology, skills, institutions). \(\alpha\) ≈ 0.3–0.4. Long-run growth = growth of the workforce + growth of productivity. With an ageing population (slower \(g_L\)), productivity is the only lasting engine — which is why the potential of AI matters so much for long-term growth forecasts.

For investors: over the long run, corporate profits grow roughly in line with nominal GDP. A country's stock market returns, however, do not follow its GDP growth closely — fast-growing economies often have expensive markets, dilution from new share issues, and companies that earn their profits abroad.

3.3 The business cycle

Economies alternate between expansion and contraction. A recession is a significant, broad decline in activity (a rule of thumb is two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP; the US uses a committee, the NBER).

  • PMI (purchasing managers' index): survey of companies; above 50 = expansion, below 50 = contraction. Published early each month — one of the most market-moving data points. Switzerland has the procure.ch PMI; the KOF Economic Barometer is another key Swiss leading indicator.
  • Sahm rule: when the 3-month average US unemployment rate rises 0.5 percentage points above its low of the previous 12 months, the economy has historically been in recession.
  • Inverted yield curve: has preceded most US recessions, with lags of 6–24 months.

3.4 Inflation

Inflation is the general rise in prices, measured by the consumer price index (CPI; in Switzerland the LIK/IPC from the Federal Statistical Office). Core inflation removes volatile food and energy. The Fed targets PCE inflation.

Causes

  • Demand-pull: too much demand for the available supply (overheating, large fiscal stimulus).
  • Cost-push: supply shocks (oil, supply chains, wages, tariffs).
  • Expectations: if people expect inflation, they ask for higher wages and firms raise prices — inflation becomes self-fulfilling. This is why central banks care so much about credibility.
  • Money: the quantity theory \(MV = PY\) (money × velocity = price level × real output): if money grows much faster than output for long, prices rise.
Formula · Expectations-augmented Phillips curve \[ \pi_t = \pi^e_t - \beta\,(u_t - u^*) + \varepsilon_t \]

Inflation equals expected inflation, plus pressure when unemployment \(u\) is below its natural rate \(u^*\) (NAIRU), plus supply shocks \(\varepsilon\). If expectations are anchored, a tight labour market raises inflation only moderately; if expectations drift up, inflation becomes much harder to bring down.

Why inflation matters for investors: it erodes fixed payments (bonds), raises rates (lower valuations), changes the stock–bond correlation (positive when inflation is the main risk), and redistributes from lenders to borrowers. Deflation is also dangerous: it raises the real burden of debt (Fisher's debt-deflation) and makes people delay purchases.

3.5 The labour market

  • Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force. Participation rate = labour force / working-age population.
  • Payrolls: the US non-farm payrolls report (first Friday of each month) is one of the biggest market events.
  • Wages: wage growth above productivity growth + inflation target puts pressure on inflation.
  • Okun's law: roughly, each 1 point of unemployment above its natural rate corresponds to about 2% of GDP below potential.

3.6 Fiscal policy and public debt

Governments influence demand with spending and taxes. In a recession, automatic stabilisers (lower taxes, higher unemployment benefits) support demand without new decisions. The fiscal multiplier (extra GDP per franc of spending) is larger in deep recessions and when interest rates are at zero; smaller when the economy is at full capacity (crowding out).

Formula · Debt dynamics \[ \Delta d_t \approx \frac{r - g}{1 + g}\,d_{t-1} - pb_t \]

\(d\) = debt/GDP, \(r\) = average interest rate on debt, \(g\) = nominal GDP growth, \(pb\) = primary balance (budget balance before interest). When \(r < g\), debt ratios can fall even with small deficits; when \(r > g\), governments need primary surpluses to stabilise debt. Example: debt 100% of GDP, \(r = 4\%\), \(g = 3\%\), balanced primary budget → debt/GDP rises by about 1 point per year.

Switzerland has a constitutional debt brake (since 2003) that limits federal spending to cyclically adjusted revenues; Swiss federal debt is low by international standards, while debt-to-GDP in the US, Japan, Italy and France is high. Large deficits can push up long-term yields (term premium) and, in extreme cases, threaten central bank independence ("fiscal dominance").

3.7 The open economy: trade, capital flows and currencies

  • Balance of payments: current account (trade + income) + financial account = 0. A current account surplus (Switzerland, Germany, China) means the country saves more than it invests and lends to the rest of the world; a deficit (US) means it borrows.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): in the long run, exchange rates tend to move so that the same basket costs the same everywhere; high-inflation currencies depreciate. The Economist's "Big Mac index" is a fun version. PPP works poorly in the short run.
  • Interest rate parity: see the Interest Rates course — rate differentials drive forward rates and carry trades.
  • The impossible trinity (Mundell–Fleming): a country cannot have at the same time a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement and an independent monetary policy — it must choose two. Switzerland's 2011–15 floor showed the cost of fighting this.
  • Safe havens: in crises, money flows into the US dollar, the Swiss franc, the Japanese yen and gold. A strong franc lowers Swiss inflation and hurts exporters — a central issue for the SNB.

3.8 Credit, debt cycles and crises

Credit lets spending grow faster than income — until debt must be repaid. Two cycles overlap (Dalio's framework):

  • Short-term debt cycle (5–10 years): central banks raise rates to cool credit, a recession follows, rates are cut, credit expands again.
  • Long-term debt cycle (50–75 years): debt rises faster than income over decades until rates hit zero and cannot be cut further; then come deleveraging, debt restructuring, money printing and sometimes inflation.

Minsky's three stages of borrowing: hedge finance (income covers interest and principal) → speculative (income covers only interest) → Ponzi (needs rising asset prices to pay interest). Long calm periods push the system towards Ponzi finance — then a small shock causes a crash (2008). Warning signs: credit growing much faster than GDP, rising asset prices financed by debt, falling lending standards, very tight credit spreads.

How a PM uses macro
  • Know the consensus: what growth, inflation and rate path are priced in (futures, breakevens, analyst forecasts)?
  • Track surprises: economic surprise indices (e.g. Citi) measure whether data beat or miss forecasts — markets react to surprises.
  • Follow the calendar: PMIs (1st business days of the month), US jobs report (first Friday), CPI (mid-month), central bank meetings, GDP releases.
  • Nowcasting: combine high-frequency data (weekly jobless claims, card spending, mobility) to estimate current growth before official GDP.
  • Stress-test the portfolio for each regime: what happens in a recession? in an inflation shock? in a strong-franc scenario?
  • Do not over-trade macro: forecasting the economy is hard and markets often move before the data. Use macro mostly to size risk and avoid big mistakes.

3.10 Long-term forces to watch

  • Demographics: ageing populations (Europe, Japan, China, Switzerland) mean slower labour-force growth, higher pension and health costs, and possibly lower neutral rates.
  • Productivity and AI: the biggest unknown for long-term growth, profits and rates.
  • Deglobalisation and tariffs: supply-chain relocation, industrial policy, higher structural costs and possibly higher inflation.
  • Energy transition: huge investment needs; demand for metals (copper, lithium, rare earths).
  • Public debt: high debt in large economies limits fiscal space and may keep term premiums higher.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. Nominal GDP grows 5% and inflation is 3%. What is real growth?

About 2%.

2. The PMI rises from 46 to 49. Is the manufacturing sector expanding?

No — below 50 it is still contracting, but more slowly. Markets often react positively because the direction is improving.

3. Debt/GDP 120%, interest rate 5%, nominal growth 3%, primary balance 0. What happens to the debt ratio?

It rises by about (5% − 3%)/1.03 × 120% ≈ 2.3 points per year.

4. Why can a strong franc be a problem for the SNB?

It lowers import prices (pushing inflation towards or below zero) and hurts Swiss exporters and tourism, slowing the economy.

5. In which regime do both stocks and bonds tend to fall, and what helps?

Stagflation (growth falling, inflation rising). Cash, commodities, gold and short-duration assets tend to do better.

6. What does the impossible trinity say?

A country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and independent monetary policy all at once.
