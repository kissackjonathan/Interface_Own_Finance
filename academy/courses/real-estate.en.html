Part 1 · In briefWhat is real estate as an investment?

Real estate is land and the buildings on it: homes, apartments, offices, shops, warehouses, hotels, data centres. It is the largest asset class in the world, bigger than all stock markets combined. An investor earns two things: income (rent) and capital gains (the property becomes worth more).

You can own it directly (buy a flat), or indirectly through listed property companies, REITs, Swiss real estate funds or private funds. Real estate is slow, illiquid, local and almost always bought with debt — which is why interest rates are its most important driver.

The one idea to remember

A building is worth its net rent divided by a yield (the cap rate). Prices therefore move for two reasons only: the rent changes (economy, supply, vacancy) or the yield investors demand changes (interest rates, risk appetite, credit). Because the yield is usually small, a small change in it moves values a lot — and leverage multiplies that again.

Part 2 · HistoryCycles, crashes and rates

  1. 1920sFlorida land boom and bust (1925–26): plots resold many times in weeks, then prices collapse — the classic speculative bubble.
  2. 1960The US Congress creates REITs: companies that own income property and pass most income to shareholders, letting ordinary investors own buildings.
  3. 1980sJapanese property bubble: land under the Tokyo Imperial Palace said to be worth more than all of California. Prices fall for about 15 years after 1991.
  4. 1989–97Swiss real estate crash: after a credit-fuelled boom, rising rates make prices fall by roughly a third in real terms; many regional banks fail (Spar- und Leihkasse Thun, 1991). Switzerland introduces Lex Koller (1983 Lex Friedrich, renamed 1997) limiting foreign purchases of residential property.
  5. 1990sModern REIT era begins (1993); listed property becomes a global asset class.
  6. 2006–09US subprime crisis: easy mortgages, securitisation and speculation; US house prices fall about 27% nationally, triggering the global financial crisis.
  7. 2008–21Zero and negative interest rates (Switzerland −0.75% from 2015) push yields to record lows and prices to record highs; Swiss "investment pressure" (Anlagenotstand) drives pension money into property.
  8. 2020–21COVID: remote work hurts offices and city-centre retail; logistics and data centres boom.
  9. 2022–23Rate shock: central banks raise rates fast; cap rates rise and commercial property values fall about 20–40% in many markets. US regional banks with property loans fail (2023); René Benko's Signa group (Austria/Germany) collapses in late 2023 — Europe's largest property bankruptcy.
  10. 2021–24China's property crisis: Evergrande and Country Garden default; housing, about a quarter of China's economy including related industries, enters a long slump.
  11. 28 Sep 2025Swiss voters approve (57.7%) abolishing the imputed rental value (Eigenmietwert / valeur locative) on owner-occupied homes, together with most deductions for mortgage interest; planned to take effect on 1 January 2029.

Part 3 · The coreWhat makes property prices rise and fall

3.1 Valuation: the cap rate formula

Formula · Direct capitalisation \[ V = \frac{NOI}{\text{cap rate}} \]

NOI (net operating income) = rents actually collected − operating costs (maintenance, management, insurance, property tax), before debt and income tax. The cap rate is the yield investors accept for that building. It is a perpetuity with growth: cap rate ≈ required return − expected rent growth \((r - g)\).

Example: the power of the cap rate

An apartment building earns NOI of CHF 500,000. At a 4% cap rate it is worth 500,000 / 0.04 = CHF 12.5 million. If cap rates rise to 4.5% (interest rates up), its value is 500,000 / 0.045 = CHF 11.1 million: −11%, with no change in the rent at all. If rents rise 10% but the cap rate goes from 4% to 4.5%, value is still 550,000/0.045 = 12.2m — still below the start.

Other valuation methods:

  • DCF: forecast rent, vacancy, costs and capex for 10 years, add an exit value, discount. Swiss valuers (Wüest Partner, CBRE, JLL) mostly use DCF.
  • Comparables: price per m² of similar recent sales — the main method for homes.
  • Replacement cost: land + construction cost. If market values are far above it, developers build, adding supply; if below, nothing gets built.
  • Gross yield = annual rent / price — quick but ignores costs and vacancy.

3.2 The drivers of the cap rate (the yield side)

  1. Interest rates — the most important. Cap rate ≈ risk-free rate + risk premium − growth. When 10-year government yields rise, investors demand higher property yields. The spread between cap rates and bond yields tells you whether property is cheap or expensive versus bonds.
  2. Mortgage availability: bank lending standards, loan-to-value limits, regulation (FINMA, Swiss capital buffers). Easier credit = higher prices.
  3. Capital flows: pension funds, insurers, sovereign funds, foreign investors. In Switzerland, pension funds' need for yield in the zero-rate era pushed cap rates to around 2% for prime apartments.
  4. Risk appetite and liquidity: in crises, buyers disappear; transaction volume collapses before prices fall.
  5. Inflation: property is a partial hedge when rents are indexed, but high inflation brings higher rates, which usually hurt more in the short term.

3.3 The drivers of rent (the income side)

  • Demand: population growth (immigration is the main driver in Switzerland), household formation (smaller households need more homes), jobs and wages, for offices: office employment; for shops: consumer spending and e-commerce; for logistics: e-commerce and supply chains.
  • Supply: construction is slow (2–5 years) and limited by zoning, land, building costs and permits. Swiss law against urban sprawl (RPG/LAT 2014) and objections slow new building. Supply that responds late creates cycles.
  • Vacancy rate: the key indicator. Swiss housing vacancy is around 1% — very low — meaning tight markets and rising rents; above about 2% rents stall.
  • Lease structure: length of leases (WAULT), indexation to inflation, tenant quality.
  • Regulation: rent control. In Switzerland, existing rents are tied to the reference mortgage rate (published quarterly by the Federal Housing Office): each +0.25 point increase allows a rent rise of 3%; each −0.25 point requires a reduction of about 2.91% if tenants ask; plus 40% of CPI inflation and cost increases.

3.4 Leverage: why property amplifies everything

Formula · Return on equity with debt \[ ROE = y + (y - i)\,\frac{D}{E} \]

where \(y\) is the property yield (unlevered return), \(i\) the interest rate on debt, \(D/E\) debt to equity. Positive leverage when \(y > i\); negative leverage when debt costs more than the property yields.

Example

Yield 4%, mortgage 2%, 70% loan-to-value (D/E = 70/30 = 2.33): ROE = 4% + (4% − 2%) × 2.33 = 8.67%. If rates rise to 5%, ROE = 4% + (−1%) × 2.33 = 1.67%. And if the price falls 10%, the equity of 30 loses 10, i.e. −33%. This is why rate shocks hit property owners so hard.

3.5 The property cycle

  1. Recovery: high vacancy, low construction, rents bottom.
  2. Expansion: vacancy falls, rents rise, developers start building.
  3. Hyper-supply: new buildings arrive when demand slows; vacancy rises.
  4. Recession: rents and values fall, distressed sales, banks tighten.

The long lag between decision and delivery of new buildings is why property cycles overshoot. Credit amplifies each phase.

3.7 Buying a home in Switzerland

  • Equity: at least 20% of the price, of which at least 10% "hard" equity (savings, 3rd pillar, gifts — not from the 2nd pillar/LPP).
  • Amortisation: the second mortgage (above 2/3 of value) must be repaid within 15 years or by retirement.
  • Affordability test (Tragbarkeit): banks calculate costs with an imputed interest rate of about 5% (not the actual rate), plus 1% maintenance, plus amortisation; total must not exceed about one third of gross income.
Example

House CHF 1.2m, mortgage 960k (80%). Imputed interest 5% × 960k = 48,000; maintenance 1% × 1.2m = 12,000; amortisation of the second tier (960k − 800k = 160k over 15 years) ≈ 10,700. Total ≈ 70,700 → required gross income ≈ 70,700 × 3 ≈ CHF 212,000.

  • Mortgage types: fixed-rate (2–15 years), SARON-linked (follows the SNB policy rate), mixed.
  • Taxes: until 2028, owners pay income tax on the imputed rental value and deduct mortgage interest and maintenance; from 2029 both disappear for main residences (cantons may introduce a tax on second homes). This favours paying down debt.
  • Lex Koller: non-residents need permission to buy residential property in Switzerland; commercial property and listed property shares are open.

3.8 Ways to invest

  • Direct ownership: control and leverage, but concentrated, illiquid, time-consuming.
  • REITs and listed property companies: liquid, diversified. Key metrics: FFO (funds from operations = net income + depreciation − property sale gains), NAV per share (value of properties − debt), premium/discount to NAV, LTV, dividend yield. Listed prices react to rates immediately, while private valuations lag by months.
  • Swiss real estate funds (e.g. UBS Sima, CS/UBS Siat, SF Sustainable Property): listed on SIX; they usually trade at a premium (agio) to NAV — 20–40% in the low-rate era, much lower in 2022–23. Funds holding properties directly have tax advantages for private investors in some cantons.
  • Swiss property companies: Swiss Prime Site, PSP Swiss Property, Allreal, Mobimo — mainly office and commercial.
  • Private funds: core, value-add, opportunistic (Blackstone, Brookfield) — higher fees, less liquidity, smoothed valuations.

3.9 Spotting a bubble

  • Price-to-income and price-to-rent ratios far above history.
  • Mortgage growth faster than income growth; rising loan-to-value and interest-only loans.
  • Buy-to-let speculation and expectations of endless price increases.
  • Construction boom and rising vacancy.
  • The UBS Swiss Real Estate Bubble Index and the UBS Global Real Estate Bubble Index track these signals.
The PM dashboard

Watch: 10-year government yields and the spread to cap rates · central bank policy (SNB, ECB, Fed) · mortgage lending growth · vacancy rates and construction permits · population growth · listed REIT discount to NAV (an early signal of private value changes) · transaction volumes · refinancing walls (debt maturing in coming years).

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. NOI 300k, cap rate 3.5%. Value? And at 4%?

8.57m; 7.5m (−12.5%).

2. Yield 5%, mortgage 3%, LTV 60%. ROE?

D/E = 1.5 → 5% + 2% × 1.5 = 8%.

3. Why do REIT prices fall before private property valuations?

Listed prices react daily to rates; private appraisals are based on past transactions and lag.

4. Why does very low vacancy push rents up?

Tenants compete for few available units, and supply cannot respond quickly.

5. What happens to Swiss rents if the reference rate goes from 1.75% to 1.50%?

Tenants can request a reduction of about 2.91%.

Part 5 · GlossaryKey terms

Agio
Premium of a real estate fund's market price over its NAV.
Cap rate
NOI divided by property value.
FFO
Funds from operations; REIT cash earnings measure.
Imputed rental value
Taxable notional rent on an owner-occupied Swiss home (to be abolished from 2029).
LTV
Loan-to-value; debt divided by property value.
NAV
Net asset value; property values minus debt.
NOI
Net operating income; rents minus operating costs.
Reference rate
Swiss average mortgage rate used to adjust existing rents.
REIT
Real estate investment trust; listed, tax-transparent property company.
SARON
Swiss Average Rate Overnight; base for variable mortgages.
Vacancy rate
Share of empty units.
WAULT
Weighted average unexpired lease term.
