Part 1 · In briefWhat is a trader?

A trader buys and sells financial instruments — stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities, derivatives — either to make a profit from price moves or to execute orders for someone else as well as possible. Where an investor asks "what is this worth over five years?", a trader asks "where will the price be in the next minutes, days or weeks — and how do I get in and out at the best price with controlled risk?"

The one idea to remember

Trading is first a risk-management job. Good traders are not right most of the time; they lose small when wrong and win bigger when right, and they never risk so much that one bad day ends their career. Discipline beats prediction.

By horizon and style

  • Scalper: seconds to minutes, many trades a day.
  • Day trader: opens and closes positions within the day; no overnight risk.
  • Swing trader: days to weeks, riding short trends.
  • Position trader: weeks to months, often combining macro or fundamental views with technical timing.
  • Discretionary vs systematic: decisions by human judgement, or by rules and algorithms.
  • Directional vs relative value: betting on up/down, or on the gap between two related prices (one bond vs another, a stock vs its sector, spot vs futures).

By asset class (bank desks)

Equities (cash, derivatives, delta-one), rates (government bonds, swaps), FX (spot, forwards, options), credit (corporate bonds, CDS), commodities (energy, metals, agriculture), volatility / exotics. Each desk has its own language, products and rhythm — FX and rates trade almost 24 hours; equities follow exchange hours.

Part 5 · KnowledgeWhat every trader must know

Market microstructure

  • Order book: list of buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks) at each price. Spread = best ask − best bid; depth = quantity available near the best prices.
  • Order types: market (immediate, at any price), limit (only at your price or better), stop (becomes a market order when a level is reached), stop-limit, iceberg (shows only part of the size), time in force (day, GTC, IOC).
  • Liquidity, slippage and market impact: large orders move the price against you. A common rule of thumb: impact ≈ \(\sigma \sqrt{Q/V}\) (volatility × square root of your size relative to daily volume).
  • Venues: exchanges, multilateral trading facilities, dark pools, over-the-counter (OTC) markets for bonds, FX and swaps. Auctions at open and close.

Derivatives basics

  • Futures and forwards: agreement to buy/sell later at a fixed price. Fair price \(F = S\,e^{(r - q)T}\) (spot × carry). Used to hedge or gain exposure with leverage; margin calls daily.
  • Options: right to buy (call) or sell (put) at a strike. Price driven by volatility.
  • The Greeks: delta (sensitivity to price), gamma (change in delta), vega (to volatility), theta (time decay), rho (to rates).
  • Swaps: exchanging cash flows (fixed vs floating rates, currencies, total return).

Risk and P&L

  • Position sizing: risk per trade (often 0.5–1% of capital), stops based on volatility (ATR) — see the Market Analysis course.
  • Limits: maximum loss per day (stop-loss limit), VaR limit, position and concentration limits. Breaking a limit can end a career.
  • P&L attribution: explain gains and losses by source (delta, gamma, vega, carry, new trades).
  • Asymmetry: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover. Cut losers, let winners run.

Psychology and ethics

  • Biases: loss aversion, revenge trading after a loss, overconfidence after a winning streak, anchoring on entry price.
  • Routine: a trading plan before the open, a journal after the close.
  • Rules: market abuse and insider trading are crimes (Switzerland: FINMA supervision; EU: MAR). Famous rogue-trader losses — Nick Leeson (Barings, 1995), Jérôme Kerviel (Société Générale, 2008, about €4.9 billion), Kweku Adoboli (UBS, 2011, about $2.3 billion) — all involved hidden positions and broken controls.
Getting into trading

Banks hire through summer internships and graduate programmes; trading firms (Optiver, Jane Street, IMC) test mental maths, probability and decision-making under pressure. Prepare: mental arithmetic, probability puzzles, market knowledge (where are rates, indices, oil today?), a view on a trade you would put on and why, and Python basics.

Part 6 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. What is the difference between a market maker and a prop trader?

A market maker provides prices to clients and earns the spread, managing the inventory; a prop trader takes positions with the firm's own capital to profit from price moves.

2. Why would a buy-side trader use a VWAP algorithm?

To buy or sell a large order gradually, in line with market volume, minimising market impact and matching the day's average price.

3. A trader wins 45% of trades, average win 2R, average loss 1R. Is the strategy profitable?

Expectancy = 0.45 × 2 − 0.55 × 1 = +0.35R per trade: yes.
