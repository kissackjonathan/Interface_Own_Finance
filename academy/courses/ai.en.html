Part 1 · In briefWhat is AI, really?

Artificial intelligence is the ability of computers to do tasks that normally need human intelligence: understanding language, recognising images, writing code, planning. Today's AI boom is driven by one family of techniques — deep learning — and especially by large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google), Llama (Meta), DeepSeek and Qwen (China) or Mistral (France).

The one idea to remember

An LLM is a giant statistical model trained on a huge amount of text to predict the next word. Doing this extremely well, with enough data and computing power, produces surprisingly general abilities. The economics of AI are therefore the economics of compute: chips, data centres and electricity — which is why trillions of dollars are being invested, and why investors ask whether it is a bubble.

Part 2 · Key termsThe vocabulary, made simple

Machine learning (ML)
Programs that learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules.
Neural network
A model made of layers of simple calculations ("neurons") connected by numbers called weights.
Deep learning
Neural networks with many layers; the basis of modern AI.
Parameters (weights)
The numbers the model learns. Frontier models have hundreds of billions to trillions.
Training
Adjusting the parameters on huge datasets; extremely expensive (months on tens of thousands of chips).
Inference
Using a trained model to answer a request. Each answer costs compute; inference now represents most of AI computing demand.
Transformer
The architecture behind all LLMs (Google, 2017). Its "attention" mechanism lets the model relate every word to every other word.
Token
A piece of a word (roughly ¾ of a word in English). Models read and write tokens; usage is priced per million tokens.
Context window
How many tokens the model can consider at once (a long document, a codebase).
Pre-training
First training phase on internet-scale text to learn language and knowledge.
Fine-tuning / RLHF
Further training to follow instructions and be helpful and safe, using human feedback (reinforcement learning from human feedback) or AI feedback.
Reasoning models
Models trained to "think" step by step before answering, spending more compute at inference for harder problems.
Foundation / frontier model
A large general model on which many applications are built; "frontier" = the most capable ones.
Open-weight vs closed
Open-weight models publish their parameters (Llama, DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral); closed models are only accessible through an API or app (GPT, Claude, Gemini).
Multimodal
Handles text, images, audio and video.
Agent
An AI that takes actions — uses tools, browses, writes and runs code — to complete multi-step tasks.
Hallucination
When a model confidently produces false information.
Scaling laws
The empirical finding that models improve predictably with more data, parameters and compute.
GPU / accelerator
Chips that do many calculations in parallel (Nvidia GPUs, Google TPUs, Amazon Trainium, AMD Instinct).
HBM
High-bandwidth memory stacked next to AI chips (SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron) — a key bottleneck.
Hyperscaler
Giant cloud providers: Amazon (AWS), Microsoft (Azure), Google (Cloud), plus Meta and Oracle for AI capacity.
Neocloud
New companies renting GPU capacity (CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda…), often financed with debt.
AGI
Artificial general intelligence: AI as capable as humans across most tasks. No agreed definition or date.

Part 3 · HistorySeventy years from theory to ChatGPT

  1. 1950Alan Turing asks "Can machines think?" and proposes the imitation game (Turing test).
  2. 1956The Dartmouth workshop (John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon) coins the term "artificial intelligence".
  3. 1958Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron, an early neural network.
  4. 1970s–80sAI winters: promises fail, funding collapses. Expert systems have a short boom.
  5. 1986Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularise backpropagation, the method used to train neural networks.
  6. 1997IBM's Deep Blue beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov.
  7. 2006Nvidia launches CUDA, making GPUs programmable for general computing — the hardware foundation of deep learning.
  8. 2009–12Fei-Fei Li's ImageNet dataset; in 2012 AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton), trained on two Nvidia GPUs, wins the image-recognition contest by a large margin. The deep learning revolution starts.
  9. 2014Google buys DeepMind (Demis Hassabis). In 2016 its AlphaGo beats Go champion Lee Sedol.
  10. 2015OpenAI is founded as a non-profit (Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Ilya Sutskever, Greg Brockman and others).
  11. 2017Google researchers publish "Attention Is All You Need" — the Transformer.
  12. 2019–20Microsoft invests in OpenAI; GPT-3 (2020) shows the power of scaling.
  13. 2021Anthropic is founded by Dario and Daniela Amodei and other former OpenAI researchers, with a focus on AI safety.
  14. 30 Nov 2022ChatGPT launches and becomes the fastest-growing consumer app in history. The generative AI boom begins.
  15. 2023–24GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Llama. Nvidia becomes one of the most valuable companies in the world. The EU adopts the AI Act (2024). Nobel Prizes go to AI pioneers: Physics to Hinton and Hopfield, Chemistry to Hassabis and Jumper (AlphaFold, protein structures).
  16. Jan 2025DeepSeek-R1, a Chinese open-weight reasoning model said to have been trained cheaply, shocks markets; Nvidia loses about $590 billion of market value in one day.
  17. 2025–26Reasoning models and agents; mega-deals for data centres (OpenAI's "Stargate"), circular financing between chipmakers, clouds and labs; private valuations of AI labs approach $1 trillion.

Part 5 · Bubble or not?Following the money

The scale of spending

  • The big US hyperscalers' capital expenditure, mostly for AI data centres, was around $400 billion in 2025. For 2026, Business Insider estimates spending by Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and SpaceX (which now includes xAI) at about $800–830 billion. Executives plan to spend more again in 2027.
  • Capex now absorbs most of these companies' operating cash flow (estimates around 90% for 2026, versus about 40% in 2023). Free cash flow has fallen sharply at several of them, and they increasingly borrow. Examples: record bond issues, Meta's $27 billion off-balance-sheet financing with Blue Owl for a data centre (2025), and heavy debt at neoclouds such as CoreWeave.
  • Valuations of the leading labs have exploded. OpenAI was valued at $852 billion after a $122 billion round in March 2026. Anthropic was valued at $965 billion after a $65 billion round in May 2026. Revenues grow very fast — tens of billions of dollars of annualised revenue — but both still spend far more than they earn on compute.

Arguments for a bubble

  • Revenue gap: in 2024 Sequoia's David Cahn asked "AI's $600 billion question". The end-user revenue needed to earn a return on the infrastructure is still far above what AI applications earn today.
  • Circular financing: chipmakers invest in labs that buy their chips. Examples: Nvidia's announced up to $100 billion investment in OpenAI (2025), AMD granting OpenAI warrants on its shares, and clouds investing in labs that commit to buying their cloud. Demand can look stronger than it really is.
  • Debt and off-balance-sheet structures: special-purpose vehicles, private credit and GPU-backed loans are spreading — reminiscent of the fibre-optic overbuild financed with debt before 2001.
  • Depreciation risk: AI chips may become obsolete in 3–5 years. If accounting assumes 6 years, profits look better than they are.
  • Concentration: a handful of AI-related stocks make up a large share of the S&P 500. A disappointment would hit index investors everywhere.
  • Prominent warnings: in October 2026 investors such as Ray Dalio publicly called it a "classic bubble". Many surveys of fund managers name AI stocks as the most crowded trade.

Arguments against (or "a bubble with real foundations")

  • Real demand and revenue: cloud growth re-accelerated (Google Cloud grew more than 60% year-on-year in early 2026). Hyperscalers say they are "capacity constrained", and AI lab revenues grow several times per year.
  • Rich buyers: unlike the dot-com start-ups, most spending comes from some of the most profitable companies in history.
  • Falling costs drive usage: the price per token has fallen dramatically, which expands adoption (agents, coding, enterprise automation). As with past technologies, cheaper units have led to more total spending.
  • Valuations are not all extreme: Nvidia's forward P/E has often been lower than that of many software companies, thanks to its earnings growth.
How a PM thinks about it

Both can be true: AI can be a transformative technology and parts of the market can be overvalued. Railways and the internet changed the world, yet many investors in them lost money because too much capacity was built too fast with too much debt. Watch: (1) revenue of AI applications vs capex, (2) GPU rental prices and utilisation, (3) debt and credit spreads of neoclouds and data-centre financings, (4) whether capex guidance keeps rising, (5) power availability. Picks-and-shovels (chips, power) win first; the long-term winners are often not the first movers.

Part 7 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. What is the difference between training and inference?

Training builds the model by adjusting its parameters on huge datasets (a big one-off cost); inference is using the model to answer requests (a recurring cost per use).

2. Why is "circular financing" a warning sign?

When suppliers invest in their own customers who then buy from them, reported demand and revenue can be inflated by the supplier's own money rather than by end-users.

3. Why did DeepSeek-R1 hit Nvidia's share price?

It suggested strong models could be built with far less compute, raising fears that demand for expensive GPUs was overestimated.
