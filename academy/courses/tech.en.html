Part 1 · In briefThe sector that eats the world

The technology sector designs and sells chips, computers, phones, software, internet platforms and cloud services. It is the largest sector of world stock markets — in the S&P 500, technology and tech-like companies (including Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, which are classified in other sectors) make up well over a third of the index. A handful of companies are each worth several trillion dollars.

The one idea to remember

Tech's economics are special: very high fixed costs, near-zero marginal costs, network effects and switching costs. Winners can scale to billions of users with enormous margins ("winner takes most"), but technology shifts (PC → internet → mobile → cloud → AI) can also destroy leaders. To analyse tech, understand where in the value chain a company sits, what locks customers in, and how exposed it is to the next shift.

Part 2 · Quick historySeventy years in ten steps

  1. 1947Transistor invented at Bell Labs (Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley).
  2. 1958–59Integrated circuit (Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments, Robert Noyce at Fairchild). Silicon Valley is born.
  3. 1965–71Moore's law (transistor counts double about every two years); Intel founded (1968); first microprocessor, the Intel 4004 (1971).
  4. 1975–84Microsoft (1975), Apple (1976), IBM PC (1981), Macintosh (1984): the PC era.
  5. 1987Morris Chang founds TSMC: chip design and manufacturing separate.
  6. 1989–95Tim Berners-Lee invents the World Wide Web at CERN in Geneva; Netscape's IPO (1995) opens the internet era. Amazon (1994), Google (1998).
  7. 2000–02Dot-com crash: the Nasdaq falls about 78%.
  8. 2004–08Facebook (2004), AWS (2006), iPhone (2007), App Store and Android (2008): mobile and cloud.
  9. 2012–20Deep learning; "FAANG" dominate; China's internet giants rise; US–China tech rivalry begins (Huawei sanctions 2019).
  10. 2022–26ChatGPT and the AI boom; Nvidia becomes the world's most valuable company; export controls on chips; hundreds of billions per year of AI capex.

Part 3 · The key companiesDeep dives into the giants

For each: how it started, what it does, how it makes money, why it wins, and what could go wrong.

Apple (USA)

Origin: founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak in a garage in California. Near-bankrupt in 1997, saved by Jobs' return, the iMac, iPod (2001) and above all the iPhone (2007). Tim Cook CEO since 2011.

Business model: premium hardware + closed ecosystem + high-margin services. The iPhone is roughly half of revenue; Services (App Store commissions, iCloud, Apple Music, AppleCare, payments from Google to be the default search engine) are about a quarter of revenue but a much larger share of gross profit, with margins around 70–75%. Apple designs its own chips (Apple silicon, made by TSMC) but outsources assembly (Foxconn, Pegatron, Luxshare, in China and increasingly India).

Why it wins: brand, an installed base of more than 2 billion active devices, ecosystem lock-in (iMessage, AirDrop, Watch, AirPods), pricing power. Returns enormous cash to shareholders through buybacks.

Risks: dependence on China (production and sales), regulation of the App Store (EU Digital Markets Act, US antitrust), possible end of Google default payments, a saturated smartphone market, being late in AI.

Microsoft (USA)

Origin: founded in 1975 by Bill Gates and Paul Allen; MS-DOS for the IBM PC, then Windows and Office dominated the PC era. Missed mobile under Steve Ballmer; reinvented by Satya Nadella (CEO since 2014) around the cloud.

Business model: three engines. (1) Productivity: Microsoft 365 subscriptions, Teams, LinkedIn (bought 2016), Dynamics. (2) Intelligent Cloud: Azure (number two cloud), servers, GitHub (2018). (3) Personal computing: Windows, Xbox, Activision Blizzard (bought 2023 for about $69 billion). Most revenue is now recurring subscriptions sold to businesses.

AI: partner and major investor in OpenAI since 2019; integrates AI "Copilots" in all products; one of the largest AI capex spenders.

Why it wins: enterprise relationships with almost every company in the world, bundling, switching costs. Risks: AI capex returns, dependence on OpenAI, cloud competition, antitrust.

Alphabet / Google (USA)

Origin: Google founded in 1998 by Stanford students Larry Page and Sergey Brin; PageRank made search far better. Restructured as Alphabet in 2015; Sundar Pichai CEO.

Business model: advertising is the majority of revenue: Search ads (advertisers pay per click on keywords) and YouTube ads (bought 2006 for $1.65 billion). Other engines: Google Cloud (fast-growing, now profitable), subscriptions (YouTube Premium, Google One), Android and Play Store, Pixel devices. "Other Bets" include Waymo (robotaxis). Owns DeepMind (Gemini models) and designs its own AI chips (TPUs, with Broadcom).

Why it wins: dominant search share, data, distribution (Android, Chrome), world-class AI research, a cost advantage from its own chips. Risks: AI chatbots changing how people search, antitrust (a US court ruled in 2024 that Google illegally monopolised search), heavy capex.

Amazon (USA)

Origin: founded in 1994 by Jeff Bezos as an online bookshop in Seattle; built on long-term thinking ("Day 1") and a "flywheel": lower prices → more customers → more sellers → more selection → scale → lower costs.

Business model: (1) e-commerce: first-party retail plus a marketplace where third-party sellers pay commissions and fulfilment fees; Prime subscription (2005). (2) AWS (2006), the largest cloud provider, which generates a majority of Amazon's operating profit. (3) Advertising on its shopping pages — fast-growing and very profitable. Plus Whole Foods (2017), Prime Video, Kuiper/Leo satellites. Big investor in Anthropic and maker of its own AI chips (Trainium).

Why it wins: logistics network nobody can match, scale, customer data. Risks: thin retail margins, antitrust, AWS competition, huge capex turning free cash flow negative.

Meta Platforms (USA)

Origin: Facebook founded in 2004 by Mark Zuckerberg at Harvard. Bought Instagram (2012, $1 billion) and WhatsApp (2014, $19 billion) — two of the best acquisitions in history. Renamed Meta in 2021.

Business model: almost all revenue is advertising shown to more than 3 billion daily users across its apps. AI-driven targeting and ranking (Reels) are key. Reality Labs (VR headsets, smart glasses, metaverse) loses tens of billions per year. Develops Llama models and spends heavily on AI data centres. Zuckerberg controls the company through super-voting shares.

Risks: no cloud business to resell its AI capacity (so AI spending must pay off through ads), regulation and privacy, teenage usage concerns, Apple/Google control of mobile platforms.

Nvidia (USA)

Origin: founded in 1993 by Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem to make graphics chips for video games. In 2006 it launched CUDA, software that lets developers use GPUs for any calculation — a bet that took a decade to pay off and became its moat when AI arrived.

Business model: fabless — designs chips, TSMC makes them. Sells complete AI systems: GPUs (Hopper, Blackwell, Rubin generations), networking (from Mellanox, bought in 2020), software and full racks. Data centre sales are now the vast majority of revenue; gross margins around 70–75%. In October 2025 it became the first company worth $5 trillion.

Why it wins: CUDA's developer ecosystem, a full-stack approach, a yearly product cadence, supply relationships (TSMC capacity, HBM). Risks: a few hyperscalers are most of demand; they are building their own chips (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, Microsoft Maia, Meta MTIA); China export restrictions; cyclicality if AI capex slows.

TSMC (Taiwan)

Origin: founded in 1987 by Morris Chang, a former Texas Instruments executive, with support from the Taiwanese government and Philips. He invented the pure-play foundry: manufacture chips for others, never design your own, so you never compete with clients.

Business model: customers (Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm, Broadcom, MediaTek) design chips; TSMC manufactures them in leading-edge processes (3nm, 2nm) and advanced packaging (CoWoS, essential for AI chips). It holds about two-thirds of the global foundry market and almost all production of the most advanced chips. Builds fabs in Arizona, Japan (Kumamoto) and Germany (Dresden).

Why it wins: decades of manufacturing excellence, yields, scale, trust. Risks: geopolitics (China–Taiwan), enormous capex, higher costs abroad, Intel and Samsung trying to catch up.

ASML (Netherlands)

Origin: created in 1984 as a joint venture of Philips and ASM International in Veldhoven. Spent two decades and billions of euros, with partners (Zeiss for optics, Cymer for the light source) and customer funding (Intel, TSMC, Samsung), to develop EUV lithography.

Business model: the only company in the world making EUV machines, which print the smallest chip features. An EUV machine costs well over €150 million, a "High-NA" EUV even more; each is the size of a bus and shipped in many containers. Recurring revenue from servicing and upgrading the installed base.

Why it wins: a true monopoly at the leading edge. Risks: cyclical customer capex, export restrictions to China (an important market for older machines), concentration on a few customers.

Samsung Electronics (South Korea)

Origin: part of the Samsung group founded in 1938 by Lee Byung-chul as a trading company; electronics from 1969; entered memory chips in the 1980s and became the world leader. Controlled by the Lee family through cross-shareholdings (a chaebol).

Business model: (1) memory (DRAM, NAND) — highly cyclical, very profitable at peaks; (2) foundry (number two after TSMC, far behind); (3) Galaxy phones; (4) displays, TVs and appliances. Lost ground to SK Hynix in HBM for AI in 2023–25 and works to catch up.

Risks: memory cycles, foundry losses, governance and a "Korea discount" on valuations.

Broadcom (USA)

Origin: today's Broadcom comes from Avago (a Hewlett-Packard chip spin-off) which, under CEO Hock Tan, bought Broadcom in 2016 and kept the name. Built by acquisitions: CA Technologies, Symantec's enterprise business, and VMware (2023, about $69 billion).

Business model: (1) semiconductors: networking chips for data centres and custom AI accelerators designed with customers (Google's TPU and others); (2) infrastructure software (VMware) sold as subscriptions with sharp price increases. Famous for cost cutting after acquisitions and high margins.

Risks: customer concentration, debt from acquisitions, VMware customer backlash.

Tencent (China)

Origin: founded in 1998 in Shenzhen by Pony Ma and partners; QQ messenger, then WeChat (2011), China's "super-app" with more than a billion users (messaging, payments, mini-programs, services).

Business model: games (the world's largest gaming company: Honor of Kings, Riot Games/League of Legends, stakes in Epic, Supercell), online advertising, fintech and business services (WeChat Pay, cloud), plus a huge portfolio of stakes in other companies.

Risks: Chinese regulation (gaming limits, 2021 crackdown), US–China tensions, Chinese consumer weakness.

Alibaba (China)

Origin: founded in 1999 by Jack Ma and 17 co-founders in Hangzhou as a B2B marketplace; Taobao (2003) beat eBay in China. Record IPO in New York in 2014 (about $25 billion). In 2020 the IPO of its affiliate Ant Group was blocked and a regulatory crackdown followed.

Business model: Taobao and Tmall marketplaces (revenue from advertising and commissions paid by merchants), Alibaba Cloud (China's largest cloud), the Qwen family of open-weight AI models, logistics (Cainiao), international commerce (AliExpress).

Risks: competition from PDD and Douyin (ByteDance), regulation, geopolitics.

SAP (Germany)

Origin: founded in 1972 in Walldorf by five former IBM engineers (including Hasso Plattner and Dietmar Hopp). Its R/3 system (1992) became the standard ERP software that runs the finance, supply chain and HR of large companies.

Business model: moving from licences + maintenance to cloud subscriptions (S/4HANA Cloud, "RISE with SAP"). Customers rarely leave: replacing an ERP is very costly and risky. Europe's most valuable software company.

Risks: execution of the cloud migration, AI changing enterprise software.

Oracle (USA)

Origin: founded in 1977 by Larry Ellison, Bob Miner and Ed Oates; built the leading relational database. Acquired PeopleSoft, Sun Microsystems (Java, 2010), NetSuite, Cerner.

Business model: database and application licences and support (very high margins), and since the AI boom a fast-growing Oracle Cloud Infrastructure with enormous multi-year contracts (OpenAI, Stargate). Financed increasingly with debt.

Risks: customer concentration on AI labs, leverage, execution of huge data-centre projects.

AMD (USA)

Origin: founded in 1969 by Jerry Sanders; for decades the "second source" of x86 processors behind Intel. Spun off its factories (GlobalFoundries, 2009). Near-collapse until Lisa Su became CEO (2014) and launched the Zen architecture (2017).

Business model: fabless CPUs (Ryzen, EPYC — large server share gains from Intel), GPUs (Radeon, Instinct AI accelerators), FPGAs from Xilinx (bought 2022), console chips. In 2025 it signed a multi-gigawatt AI chip deal with OpenAI.

Risks: Nvidia's software lead, cyclical PC market.

Netflix (USA)

Origin: founded in 1997 by Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph to rent DVDs by mail; launched streaming in 2007; original content from 2013 (House of Cards). Killed Blockbuster.

Business model: global subscriptions (more than 300 million members), an advertising tier, a crackdown on password sharing (2023). Spends heavily on content but enjoys scale economies: costs are spread over the world's largest base.

Risks: content costs, competition for attention (YouTube, TikTok, games).

Tesla (USA)

Origin: founded in 2003 by Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning; Elon Musk led the early funding and became CEO in 2008. Roadster, Model S (2012), Model 3/Y made EVs mass-market.

Business model: electric vehicles (most revenue), energy storage (Megapack, fast-growing), software (Full Self-Driving), and long-term bets on robotaxis and humanoid robots (Optimus). Valued far above traditional carmakers because investors price these bets.

Risks: Chinese competition (BYD), price cuts, key-person risk, execution of autonomy promises.

Part 4 · The geography of techThe countries that matter — and the rising ones

United States — the leader

Dominates software, internet platforms, cloud, chip design (Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm, Broadcom), AI labs and venture capital. Strengths: the best universities, deep capital markets, immigration of talent. The CHIPS Act (2022, about $52 billion) brings manufacturing back (TSMC, Samsung, Intel and Micron fabs). Weaknesses: manufacturing depends on Asia.

Taiwan — the factory of the digital world

The Hsinchu Science Park (1980) and TSMC turned Taiwan into the centre of advanced chip manufacturing. Beyond TSMC: MediaTek (smartphone chips), UMC (mature foundry), ASE (world's largest chip packaging and testing), and the contract manufacturers (Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron, Wiwynn, Inventec) that assemble most of the world's AI servers. Risk: tension with China ("silicon shield" vs invasion risk).

South Korea — memory and chaebols

Korea dominates memory: SK Hynix leads high-bandwidth memory (HBM) for AI, and Samsung is the largest DRAM maker. Strong in displays (Samsung Display, LG Display), batteries (LG Energy Solution, Samsung SDI, SK On) and internet (Naver, Kakao). The government plans giant chip clusters around Yongin. Watch the "Korea discount" — governance reforms (the "Value-up" programme) aim to reduce it.

Japan — the hidden champion of equipment and materials

Japan lost the consumer electronics race but dominates niches nobody can make without: Tokyo Electron (etch and deposition), Advantest (chip testers), Lasertec (mask inspection), Disco (wafer dicing and grinding), Shin-Etsu and SUMCO (silicon wafers — together about half the world market), JSR and Tokyo Ohka (photoresists). Sony leads image sensors. The state-backed Rapidus aims to produce 2nm chips from 2027; TSMC opened a fab in Kumamoto.

China — scale and self-sufficiency

Giant internet platforms (Tencent, Alibaba, ByteDance, Meituan, PDD, JD.com, Baidu), hardware (Huawei, Xiaomi, Lenovo), electric vehicles and batteries (BYD, CATL). Since US export controls (2019 on Huawei, October 2022 on advanced chips and tools), China invests massively in self-sufficiency: SMIC (foundry), Hua Hong, Naura and AMEC (equipment), CXMT and YMTC (memory), Huawei Ascend AI chips, Cambricon. Risks for investors: regulation, geopolitics, sanctions, variable interest entity structures.

Europe — critical niches

Not a platform giant, but irreplaceable in parts of the chain: ASML, ASM International, BE Semiconductor (Netherlands), Zeiss (optics, private), Infineon (power chips, Germany), STMicroelectronics (headquartered in Geneva), NXP (auto chips), SAP, Dassault Systèmes, Adyen, Spotify. The EU Chips Act (2023) aims to double Europe's share of production.

Switzerland — small but present

STMicroelectronics (HQ Geneva), Logitech (peripherals), VAT Group (vacuum valves used in almost every chip fab — world leader), Comet (X-ray and RF power for chip-making), Inficon (sensors for fabs), Sensirion (sensors), Temenos (banking software), SoftwareOne. Plus world-class research at ETH Zurich and EPFL, Google's largest engineering office outside the US in Zurich, and CERN, birthplace of the web.

Israel — the start-up nation

Highest density of start-ups and R&D centres (Intel, Nvidia, Apple, Google). Leader in cybersecurity (Check Point, CyberArk, Wiz — Google agreed to buy Wiz for $32 billion in 2025), plus Mobileye (autonomous driving), Wix, Monday.com, NICE.

India — IT services and the next manufacturing base

World leader in IT outsourcing (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra), world-class digital public infrastructure (UPI payments). Now a fast-growing electronics manufacturer: a large and rising share of iPhones are assembled in India (Foxconn, Tata Electronics). Its semiconductor mission backs a Tata–PSMC fab (Gujarat) and Micron's assembly and test plant.

Vietnam — the "China + 1" assembly hub

Low costs, young workforce and trade agreements made Vietnam a major electronics exporter. Samsung makes around half of its smartphones there; Intel runs its largest assembly-and-test plant in Ho Chi Minh City; Foxconn, Luxshare, Goertek and Amkor (chip packaging plant near Hanoi) have expanded. Local champions: FPT Corporation (IT services, software, chip design) and the state-owned Viettel. Risks: US tariffs, infrastructure and power limits.

Malaysia — back-end semiconductor hub

Penang has been a chip assembly and test centre since the 1970s and handles roughly 13% of global back-end activity: Intel (packaging), Infineon (one of the world's largest silicon-carbide power-chip fabs in Kulim), Micron, plus local firms Inari Amertron, ViTrox, Frontken. Johor is booming with data centres serving Singapore.

Singapore and others

Singapore: fabs of GlobalFoundries, UMC, Micron; headquarters of Sea (Shopee, Garena) and Grab. Thailand (hard disks, electronics), Philippines (back-end), Indonesia (huge digital consumer market: GoTo), and the Gulf states (AI data centres) are other names to watch.

How to analyse a tech company
  1. Position in the value chain and who captures the profits (today: chips and clouds).
  2. Moat: network effects, ecosystem, switching costs, scale, IP, manufacturing know-how.
  3. Unit economics: gross margin, customer acquisition cost, retention (net revenue retention for software), lifetime value.
  4. Growth quality: organic vs acquired; "rule of 40" (growth % + free-cash-flow margin % ≥ 40) for software.
  5. Capex intensity and cyclicality: semis and memory are cyclical; inventory cycles matter.
  6. Stock-based compensation: a real cost — check dilution.
  7. Exposure to the next shift: is AI a tailwind or a threat (e.g. seat-based software, search)?
  8. Geopolitics: China revenue, Taiwan supply chain, export controls, tariffs.

Part 6 · Check yourselfQuestions

1. Why is TSMC's "pure-play foundry" model powerful?

It never competes with its customers, so every chip designer can trust it; this concentrates volume, learning and capital in one company, creating a scale and know-how lead nobody can match.

2. What makes Nvidia hard to displace, and what is its biggest risk?

CUDA's software ecosystem and full-system approach. Biggest risks: concentrated customers building their own chips, and a slowdown in AI capex.

3. Name three countries benefiting from "China + 1" supply-chain diversification.

Vietnam, India, Malaysia (also Mexico, Thailand).

4. Which Swiss companies are part of the semiconductor supply chain?

STMicroelectronics (HQ Geneva), VAT Group, Comet, Inficon (and Sensirion for sensors).
