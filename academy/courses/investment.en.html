Part 1 · In briefWhat investing really is: return, risk and skill

Investing means giving up money today to get more money later. The "more" is the return. The fact that you do not know exactly how much you will get is the risk. Every concept a portfolio manager uses — alpha, beta, Sharpe ratio, diversification, tracking error — is a way of measuring, separating or controlling these two things.

The single most important idea of this course is this: the return of any portfolio can be split into two parts.

The one idea to remember

Beta is the part of your return that comes from being exposed to the market (or to other broad, well-known sources of return). You can get it almost for free with an index fund.
Alpha is the part that comes from skill: picking the right assets at the right time. It is rare, hard to prove, and it is the only thing worth paying a manager high fees for.

A simple picture: you are on a boat in a river. The river's current is beta — it moves every boat. How well you row is alpha. If you arrive first only because you were in the fastest part of the current, you are not a better rower. A portfolio manager's job is to row well, to know how much current they are using, and to not sink in the rapids (risk management).

Three more ideas complete the picture:

  • Risk and return are linked. In a competitive market, higher expected returns usually come with higher risk. If someone offers high return with no risk, be very suspicious.
  • Diversification is the only free lunch. Combining assets that do not move together reduces risk without reducing expected return.
  • What matters is risk-adjusted return. 12% with wild swings can be worse than 8% with calm, steady growth — especially if you can use leverage or if you might need the money at a bad moment.

Key dates

  2. 1738Daniel Bernoulli explains that people care about the utility of money, not the money itself: losing 1,000 hurts more than winning 1,000 pleases. The root of risk aversion.

  4. 1921Frank Knight separates risk (odds you can measure) from uncertainty (odds you cannot know). Markets are full of both.

  6. 1958James Tobin shows the separation theorem: everyone should hold the same best risky portfolio, mixed with cash or borrowing according to their taste for risk.
  7. 1964William Sharpe publishes the CAPM (with parallel work by Lintner, Treynor and Mossin). Beta becomes the measure of systematic risk.
  8. 1965–68Jack Treynor (1965) proposes a return-per-beta ratio; Sharpe (1966) the return-per-volatility "reward-to-variability" ratio; Michael Jensen (1968) defines alpha and finds that most mutual funds did not beat the market.
  9. 1973Black, Scholes and Merton publish the option pricing model. Risk can now be priced, sliced and transferred.
  10. 1975–76John Bogle founds Vanguard and launches the first index fund for retail investors. Cheap beta for everyone.

  12. 1986Brinson, Hood and Beebower find that asset allocation explains most of the variation of pension fund returns over time. Allocation becomes the first decision.

  21. 2009Cremers and Petajisto introduce active share: how different a fund really is from its index. Many "active" funds are closet index funds.

What history teaches a PM

The tools became more precise, but each crisis showed their limits: volatility is not the only risk, correlations change, leverage kills, and much of what looks like skill is exposure to known factors. A great PM uses the formulas and stays humble about them.

Part 3 · The coreThe machinery of return, risk and skill

3.1 Measuring return correctly

Simple and log returns

\[ R_t = \frac{P_t + D_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \qquad\qquad r_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_t + D_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) \]

The simple return \(R\) includes price change and dividends \(D\) (this is the total return). The log return \(r\) is useful because log returns add up over time: the return over a year is the sum of the daily log returns. For small moves, both are almost equal.

Arithmetic vs geometric average, and volatility drag

If a stock goes +50% then −50%, the arithmetic average is 0%. But 100 → 150 → 75: you lost 25%. The geometric average (the true compound growth rate) is \(\sqrt{1.5 \times 0.5} - 1 = -13.4\%\) per period.

Formulas · Compound growth \[ G = \left[\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1+R_t)\right]^{1/T} - 1 \qquad\qquad G \approx A - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \]

\(A\) = arithmetic mean, \(\sigma\) = volatility. The gap \(\sigma^2/2\) is the volatility drag: for the same average return, a more volatile investment grows more slowly. With 20% volatility, the drag is about 2% per year. This is why reducing volatility (through diversification) directly increases long-term wealth.

Annualising

Returns compound: a monthly return of 1% is \((1.01)^{12} - 1 = 12.7\%\) per year. Volatility scales with the square root of time (if returns are independent): \(\sigma_{\text{year}} = \sigma_{\text{day}}\sqrt{252} = \sigma_{\text{month}}\sqrt{12}\).

Nominal vs real return

What matters is purchasing power. Irving Fisher's relation:

\[ (1 + R_{\text{nominal}}) = (1 + R_{\text{real}})(1 + \pi) \quad\Rightarrow\quad R_{\text{real}} \approx R_{\text{nominal}} - \pi \]

where \(\pi\) is inflation. A 5% return with 3% inflation is only about 2% real.

Currency: returns for a Swiss investor

If you own a US stock, your return in francs depends on the stock and on the dollar:

\[ 1 + R_{\text{CHF}} = (1 + R_{\text{USD}})(1 + R_{\text{USD/CHF}}) \]
Example

Your US stock gains 10% in dollars, but the dollar loses 5% against the franc. Your return in CHF is \(1.10 \times 0.95 - 1 = 4.5\%\). Over the long run, the franc has tended to appreciate against most currencies, so unhedged foreign assets have often returned less in CHF than in local currency. Hedging removes this effect but has a cost (or gain) roughly equal to the interest rate difference between the two currencies.

Time-weighted vs money-weighted return

Time-weighted return (TWR) measures the manager's performance, ignoring when clients add or withdraw money — it is the standard for comparing funds (GIPS standard). Money-weighted return (MWR), which is the internal rate of return (IRR) of your cash flows, measures your experience. If you invest a lot just before a crash, your MWR will be much lower than the fund's TWR.

3.2 Measuring risk

Risk is the possibility that results are different from what you expect — mostly, worse. No single number captures it, so professionals use several.

Volatility

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(R_t - \bar R)^2} \]

The standard deviation of returns. Long-run equity market volatility is around 15–20% per year; government bonds 4–8%; a single stock often 25–40%. Volatility treats up and down moves the same and assumes they are "normal" — two big simplifications.

Downside measures

  • Downside deviation: volatility of returns below a target (often 0 or the risk-free rate). Used in the Sortino ratio.
  • Maximum drawdown: the largest fall from a peak to a following low. World stocks fell roughly 55–60% in 2007–09. A 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover — the asymmetry of losses.
  • Time to recovery: how long it takes to get back to the previous peak.

Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall

Formulas · VaR and ES \[ \text{VaR}_{\alpha} = z_{\alpha} \times \sigma \times V \qquad\qquad \text{ES}_{\alpha} = \mathbb{E}\left[\,\text{Loss} \mid \text{Loss} > \text{VaR}_{\alpha}\right] \]

VaR answers: "What is the loss I should not exceed with probability \(\alpha\) over a given horizon?" \(z_{95\%} = 1.645\), \(z_{99\%} = 2.326\) for a normal distribution, \(V\) = portfolio value. Expected Shortfall (or CVaR) answers the better question: "When things go beyond VaR, how bad is it on average?" Bank regulation (Basel) moved from VaR to ES for this reason.

Example

Portfolio of CHF 100,000 with 15% annual volatility. Daily volatility = 15% / √252 = 0.945%. One-day 95% VaR = 1.645 × 0.945% × 100,000 ≈ CHF 1,554. One-day 99% VaR ≈ CHF 2,198. In plain words: on about 1 day in 20 you should expect to lose more than CHF 1,554.

Fat tails, skewness and kurtosis

Real returns are not normal. Skewness measures asymmetry (stock markets have negative skew: crashes are bigger than rallies). Kurtosis measures fat tails: extreme days happen far more often than a normal curve predicts. The 19 October 1987 crash (−22.6% in one day for the Dow) should be practically impossible under a normal distribution. So VaR based on normality underestimates the risk of disasters. Professionals add stress tests (replay 2008, 2020, 2022) and scenario analysis.

Other risks that volatility misses

  • Liquidity risk: not being able to sell without crashing the price.
  • Leverage and margin risk: being forced to sell at the worst moment.
  • Concentration risk: too much in one name, sector or theme.
  • Model risk: your risk model is wrong.
  • Permanent loss of capital: Buffett's definition of risk — the price falls and never comes back (fraud, bankruptcy, disruption).

3.3 Diversification and correlation

Correlation \(\rho\) measures how two assets move together, from −1 (opposite) to +1 (identical). Covariance is \(\text{Cov}(A,B) = \rho_{AB}\,\sigma_A\,\sigma_B\).

Formula · Volatility of a two-asset portfolio \[ \sigma_p^2 = w_A^2\sigma_A^2 + w_B^2\sigma_B^2 + 2\,w_A w_B\,\rho_{AB}\,\sigma_A\sigma_B \]

Unless \(\rho = 1\), the portfolio volatility is lower than the weighted average of the two volatilities. That gap is the benefit of diversification.

From 2000 to 2020, stocks and bonds were often negatively correlated, which made 60/40 portfolios very efficient. In 2022, with high inflation, both fell together. Correlation depends on the economic regime — especially on inflation.

Systematic vs specific risk

With \(N\) assets of equal weight, portfolio variance becomes:

\[ \sigma_p^2 = \frac{1}{N}\,\overline{\sigma^2} + \left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right)\overline{\text{Cov}} \]

As \(N\) grows, the first term (the specific or idiosyncratic risk of each company) disappears. The second term (the average covariance — systematic or market risk) stays. With 20–30 stocks from different sectors, most specific risk is gone. This is why the market only pays you for systematic risk: specific risk can be removed for free, so nobody is rewarded for holding it.

Diversification fails when you need it most

In crises, investors sell everything and correlations between risky assets rise towards 1. True protection comes from assets with a different economic driver (high-quality government bonds in deflationary crises, gold, some trend-following strategies, cash) — not from owning 200 stocks instead of 50.

3.4 The efficient frontier and the best risky portfolio

Markowitz asked: for every level of risk, which portfolio gives the highest expected return? The answer is a curve called the efficient frontier. Any portfolio below it is inefficient: you could get more return for the same risk.

  • The left tip of the curve is the global minimum variance portfolio.
  • When you add a risk-free asset (cash at rate \(r_f\)), the best combination is a straight line from \(r_f\) touching the frontier. The touching point is the tangency portfolio: the risky portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio.
  • Tobin's separation: every investor should hold this same tangency portfolio and adjust risk only by adding cash (less risk) or borrowing (more risk).
Formula · Capital Market Line \[ \mathbb{E}[R_p] = r_f + \frac{\mathbb{E}[R_M] - r_f}{\sigma_M}\,\sigma_p \]

The slope is the Sharpe ratio of the market portfolio: the "price" of one unit of volatility.

Optimisers in practice

Mean-variance optimisation is very sensitive to expected returns, which are impossible to estimate precisely. Small input errors produce extreme, concentrated portfolios — the optimiser becomes an "error maximiser" (Michaud). Solutions used by professionals: constraints (min/max weights), shrinkage of estimates, the Black-Litterman model (Goldman Sachs, 1990–92), which starts from market weights and tilts them according to the manager's views, and risk-based methods like risk parity.

3.5 Beta and the CAPM

If specific risk can be diversified away, the only risk that deserves a reward is the part linked to the market. The CAPM measures it with beta:

Formulas · Beta and the Security Market Line \[ \beta_i = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_M)}{\text{Var}(R_M)} = \rho_{i,M}\,\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_M} \qquad\qquad \mathbb{E}[R_i] = r_f + \beta_i\left(\mathbb{E}[R_M] - r_f\right) \]

The second equation is the Security Market Line: expected return rises linearly with beta. The beta of a portfolio is the weighted average of the betas: \(\beta_p = \sum_i w_i \beta_i\).

How to read beta:

  • β = 1: moves with the market on average. β = 1.4: if the market falls 10%, the stock tends to fall 14% (tech, banks, small caps). β = 0.5: defensive (utilities, consumer staples, health care).
  • A stock can have high volatility and low beta, if most of its moves are specific (a biotech waiting for a drug approval).
  • Estimation: usually a regression on 2–5 years of weekly or monthly returns against a broad index. Because measured betas tend to move towards 1 over time, providers use adjusted beta (Blume): \(\beta_{\text{adj}} = 0.67\,\beta + 0.33\).

Limits of the CAPM

  • Roll's critique: the true market portfolio (all assets in the world) is unobservable.
  • The low-beta anomaly: in practice, the security market line is too flat. Low-beta stocks have earned more than the CAPM predicts and high-beta stocks less — partly because many investors cannot borrow, so they buy high-beta stocks to get more risk (Frazzini & Pedersen, "Betting Against Beta", 2014).
  • Other factors (size, value, momentum, quality) explain returns that beta does not.

Despite this, beta stays the common language of the industry, and CAPM is still the standard way to estimate the cost of equity.

3.6 Alpha: the return of skill

Formula · Jensen's alpha \[ \alpha_p = R_p - \left[\,r_f + \beta_p\,(R_M - r_f)\,\right] \]

Alpha is the return above what the portfolio's market exposure alone would have given. In a regression \(R_p - r_f = \alpha + \beta(R_M - r_f) + \varepsilon\), alpha is the intercept.

Important points every PM must understand:

  • Alpha depends on the model. Against the CAPM a value manager may show alpha; against a model with a value factor, the same "alpha" becomes beta. The more factors you control for, the harder it is to show true alpha.
  • Active management is a zero-sum game before costs (Sharpe, 1991). All investors together hold the market. If some beat it, others must lose by the same amount. After fees and trading costs, the average active investor must underperform. This is the mathematical case for index funds.
  • Statistical significance: with 15% tracking error, even 3% alpha per year needs many years to be distinguished from luck. The \(t\)-statistic of alpha ≈ IR × \(\sqrt{\text{years}}\). For \(t = 2\) with IR = 0.5, you need 16 years of data.
  • Persistence: studies (Carhart 1997, S&P SPIVA reports) show that past winners rarely stay winners; poor performance and high fees persist more than good performance.
  • Active share (Cremers & Petajisto, 2009): \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_i |w_{p,i} - w_{b,i}|\). Below ~60%, a fund is close to its index; it can hardly produce alpha but still charges active fees ("closet indexer").
Separating alpha and beta

Modern institutions think in layers and pay for each at the right price: market beta (index funds, a few basis points), alternative or smart beta (factor strategies: value, momentum, carry — cheap and systematic), and true alpha (skill that cannot be replicated — the only thing that justifies high fees). Portable alpha means taking alpha from one strategy (e.g. a market-neutral hedge fund) and adding market beta cheaply with futures.

3.7 Multi-factor models: beta becomes plural

Ross's Arbitrage Pricing Theory (1976) says expected returns depend on exposure to several systematic factors:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_i] = r_f + \beta_{i,1}\lambda_1 + \beta_{i,2}\lambda_2 + \dots + \beta_{i,K}\lambda_K \]

\(\beta_{i,k}\) = sensitivity of asset \(i\) to factor \(k\); \(\lambda_k\) = premium paid for that factor. Factors can be macroeconomic (growth, inflation, interest rates, credit spreads, oil) or style (value, size, momentum, quality, low volatility). Commercial risk models (MSCI Barra, Axioma) decompose every portfolio into dozens of such exposures, so a PM knows exactly which bets they are making — intended or not.

PM practice

Before a meeting, a PM should be able to say: "My portfolio has a beta of 0.9, a positive quality tilt, a slight negative momentum exposure, 4% tracking error, and 70% of my active risk comes from stock-specific bets." If most of the active risk comes from unintended factor bets, the portfolio is not expressing the manager's ideas.

3.9 Benchmarks and performance attribution

A benchmark must be: investable, known in advance, representative of the manager's universe, and measurable (CFA "SAMURAI" properties). Comparing a Swiss small-cap fund with the SMI, or a global fund with the S&P 500, gives meaningless results.

Brinson attribution explains where the active return comes from. For each sector \(i\), with portfolio weights \(w_p\), benchmark weights \(w_b\), portfolio returns \(R_p\) and benchmark returns \(R_b\):

Formulas · Brinson-Fachler attribution \[ \text{Allocation}_i = (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})(R_{b,i} - R_b) \] \[ \text{Selection}_i = w_{b,i}\,(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i}) \] \[ \text{Interaction}_i = (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i}) \]

Allocation: did you overweight sectors that beat the benchmark? Selection: inside each sector, did your stocks beat the sector? Interaction: the combined effect. The three sum to the total active return.

3.10 Building a portfolio

The hierarchy of decisions

  1. Investment policy: objective, horizon, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, constraints (taxes, laws, ethics). Written in an Investment Policy Statement.
  2. Strategic asset allocation (SAA): the long-term mix (e.g. 60% equities, 30% bonds, 10% alternatives). The biggest driver of risk and return.
  3. Tactical asset allocation (TAA): temporary deviations based on valuation, cycle or momentum.
  4. Security selection inside each asset class.
  5. Implementation: vehicles (ETFs, futures, single securities), costs, taxes, currency hedging.
  6. Monitoring and rebalancing.

Risk budgeting and risk parity

A 60/40 portfolio looks balanced in francs, but about 90% of its risk comes from equities. Risk budgeting allocates risk, not capital. The contribution of asset \(i\) to portfolio risk is:

\[ \text{RC}_i = w_i\,\frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i} = w_i\,\frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p}, \qquad \sum_i \text{RC}_i = \sigma_p \]

Risk parity sets each asset's risk contribution equal. A simple version ignores correlations and uses weights proportional to \(1/\sigma_i\). Because bonds are less volatile, risk parity holds many more bonds than 60/40 and often uses leverage to reach a target return.

Rebalancing

Markets move weights away from the target. Rebalancing (by calendar, e.g. quarterly, or by thresholds, e.g. ±5%) keeps risk under control and mechanically "sells high and buys low". It costs trading fees and taxes, so bands are usually better than very frequent rebalancing.

Leverage

Borrowing to invest multiplies both return and risk: with leverage \(L\), \(R = L \cdot R_{\text{assets}} - (L-1)\,r_{\text{borrow}}\). It also increases volatility drag and creates the risk of forced selling. The Sharpe ratio is unchanged by leverage (if you borrow at the risk-free rate), which is why high-Sharpe, low-return strategies are often levered.

3.11 Costs: the only certain return

Returns are uncertain; costs are certain. Costs include management fees (TER), trading costs (spreads, commissions, market impact), taxes (Swiss stamp duty on trades, withholding taxes on foreign dividends), and currency conversion fees.

Example: the power of fees

CHF 100,000 invested for 30 years at 6% grows to CHF 574,349. With 1.5% annual fees (net 4.5%), it grows to only CHF 374,532. The fees took about 35% of the final wealth. Hedge fund "2 and 20" (2% management fee + 20% of profits) needs very large gross alpha to leave anything for the investor.

3.12 Risk tolerance and investor behaviour

  • Ability vs willingness to take risk: a 25-year-old with stable income has a high ability to take risk (long horizon, human capital) but may have a low willingness. The portfolio must respect the lower of the two.
  • Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979): losses hurt about twice as much as gains please. Investors who check their portfolio too often feel more losses and take less risk than is good for them ("myopic loss aversion", Benartzi & Thaler 1995).
  • The behaviour gap: because investors buy after rallies and sell after crashes, their money-weighted returns are often well below the time-weighted returns of the funds they own.
  • Sequence-of-returns risk: for someone withdrawing money (retirement), bad returns early do much more damage than the same returns later.

Part 4 · Check yourselfQuestions to test your understanding

1. A fund returned 15% when the market returned 12%. Its beta is 1.3 and the risk-free rate is 2%. What is its Jensen's alpha?

Expected = 2% + 1.3 × (12% − 2%) = 15%. Alpha = 15% − 15% = 0. All the outperformance came from taking more market risk.

2. Why does the market not reward specific (idiosyncratic) risk?

Because it can be removed for free by diversification. Investors who hold it voluntarily take a risk that others do not need to take, so competition does not pay a premium for it.

3. Two investments have the same 8% arithmetic average return. One has 10% volatility, the other 30%. Which grows more over the long run?

The 10% volatility one. Geometric growth ≈ 8% − 0.10²/2 = 7.5% vs 8% − 0.30²/2 = 3.5%.

4. When should you use the Treynor ratio instead of the Sharpe ratio?

When the portfolio is one part of a larger, well-diversified portfolio: its specific risk will be diversified away, so only its beta (market risk) matters.

5. A manager has an information ratio of 0.4. How many years of results do you need for a t-statistic of 2?

t ≈ IR × √years → √years = 2 / 0.4 = 5 → 25 years. This is why proving skill is so hard.

6. Your 60/40 portfolio has equities with 16% volatility and bonds with 6%. Roughly what share of the risk comes from equities?

Most of it — about 90%. Equity weight × volatility (0.6 × 16% = 9.6%) is four times the bond part (0.4 × 6% = 2.4%), and correlations make the gap even bigger in risk contributions.

7. Why is the 1-day 99% VaR from a normal model probably too optimistic?

Real returns have fat tails and negative skew; extreme losses happen more often than the normal distribution predicts. Complement it with expected shortfall and stress tests.
